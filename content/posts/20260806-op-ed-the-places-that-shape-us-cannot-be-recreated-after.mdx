The thing about a place that shapes you is that you can't build it back once it's gone. That's the argument Deb Haaland, the former Interior Secretary, is making about what happens when the Trump administration opens national monuments to mining, drilling, and logging. It's not abstract. It's not about preserving rocks for tourists. It's about the fact that some landscapes hold specific weight in people's lives, in cultures, in history. And once you tear into them for profit, the damage isn't reversible by committee.

Haaland, the first Native American to lead the Department of Interior, is writing from a position most people never occupy. She's watched administrations treat monuments as political property to be expanded or shrunk based on who's in power. The Bears Ears in Utah, the Orquista in New Mexico, Grand Staircase-Escalante. These aren't just beautiful places. They're archaeological sites, sacred grounds, irreplaceable repositories of human heritage. When you open them to resource extraction, you're not just changing the view. You're erasing evidence, destroying ecosystems, and signaling that the short-term value of minerals in the ground matters more than the long-term survival of what makes a place sacred to the people who've been stewards of it.

The real cost isn't something you can calculate in quarterly reports. It's measured in what your grandkid can't see, what they'll never know existed because it was already stripped away for copper or coal. And that's a loss nobody can fix later by drawing new lines on a map or writing new legislation. Some things, once gone, stay gone.
