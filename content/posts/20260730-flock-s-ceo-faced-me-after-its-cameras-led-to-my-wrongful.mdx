Joel Feder got stopped by cops based on a plate-reading camera system that was wrong. Flock Safety runs thousands of these AI cameras across the country, and they flagged his car as stolen when it wasn't. He sat down with CEO Garrett Langley to talk about why that happened and what comes next.

Here's the thing: Flock's cameras are everywhere now. They're mounted on utility poles, in parking lots, at gas stations. They read license plates and run them against databases of stolen vehicles, wanted suspects, and Amber Alerts. The system sounds reasonable in theory. In practice, it's feeding data into police departments with minimal guardrails, and when the AI gets it wrong, you get pulled over at gunpoint for a crime you didn't commit.

Feder's stop wasn't an isolated glitch. Flock's own data shows hundreds of false matches happening across their network, and those false matches are becoming police stops. The company has built something genuinely useful for law enforcement and public safety, but the infrastructure around it, the verification process, the accountability when things go sideways, all of it is still catching up to the scale of what they've deployed. Langley saying he wants zero wrongful stops is the right statement. Getting there means admitting the current system isn't equipped to handle it yet.
