Spokane just got a hard lesson in what happens when a wildfire finds the right conditions at the right time. The Spokane Valley Fire blew up from nothing to 200,000 acres in less than 48 hours this summer, forcing evacuations across the region and turning the sky orange in the middle of the day. That's not a freak incident anymore. That's the new baseline.

Here's the math that matters: rising temperatures mean the ground stays drier longer. Drier fuel means fires don't just burn hotter, they move faster and spread wider. Sachi Kitajima Mulkey's reporting from Wired lays out the second half of the equation too—more people have moved into the wildland-urban interface over the last two decades, meaning more homes sitting right next to the exact terrain that catches fire first. When those two things collide, you don't get a contained brush fire. You get a chain reaction that can outpace evacuation.

The Spokane fire moved so fast that people in some neighborhoods had hours, not days, to pack and leave. Some had less. The infrastructure firefighters normally rely on to stage and attack a fire couldn't keep up with the speed. It's a physics problem disguised as a weather problem. Higher heat, lower humidity, more houses in the path, and fires that accelerate to speeds nobody planned for. That's not a worst-case scenario playing out once every hundred years. It's the new normal playing out every summer now, in places that thought they were safe.
