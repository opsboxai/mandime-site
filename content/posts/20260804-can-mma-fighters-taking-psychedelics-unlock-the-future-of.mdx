MMA fighters are flying to Colombia to drink ayahuasca. That's not the start of a joke. It's the start of what might be one of the more interesting—and yes, unconventional—conversations happening right now about what actually works for traumatic brain injury.

Ten combat athletes with documented TBI went to Medellín for eight days of psychedelic-assisted treatment. These aren't weekend adventurers chasing a spiritual experience. They're people whose brains have been systematically damaged by their profession, looking for something conventional medicine hasn't given them. The angle here is neuroplasticity. Psychedelics like ayahuasca appear to increase the brain's ability to form new neural pathways, rewire damaged circuitry, and essentially rebuild itself. For someone whose repeated concussions have left them with cognitive fog, mood issues, or memory problems, that's not theoretical. That's the difference between a functional life and a struggling one.

The reason this matters beyond MMA is straightforward. Millions of people live with TBI from car accidents, falls, military service, contact sports. Conventional treatment options are limited. You get physical therapy, maybe cognitive rehab, probably some pharmaceuticals. But you rarely get the kind of fundamental brain reorganization that psychedelics seem to trigger. Research is still early, and there's legitimate caution warranted here. But the fact that high-level athletes are willing to travel internationally and take real risks on this suggests they've already exhausted what the standard playbook offers. That's worth paying attention to.
