Google just raised its capital spending ceiling to $205 billion, and Wall Street finally woke up to what AI actually costs. This isn't theoretical anymore. These are real dollars, flowing out the door, year after year, and the company basically admitted the previous estimate was already outdated before it left their mouth.

The math gets uncomfortable fast. $205 billion doesn't buy you a product that works better than GPT-4. It doesn't guarantee you'll catch up to OpenAI or beat Claude. It's the price of admission to the table where those bets are being made. And Google, with all its search revenue and cloud infrastructure, is still throwing money at the wall to see what sticks. Everyone else is smaller.

This is why the stock market got nervous. For the past couple of years, AI spending was treated as invisible. It was capex, sure, but it was capex with a promise attached: massive returns, dominance, the future. Now investors are asking the question they should have asked two years ago: what if it just costs this much forever, with no guaranteed payoff? What if the math never works? Google's been profitable forever, so it can burn cash on R&D and live to fight another day. Most companies can't. And that's the tell. When the company with the deepest pockets starts being honest about how much this costs, everyone else starts doing the math on their own balance sheets. Turns out AI's not cheap. And nobody knows if it's worth it yet.
