Tesla's Cybercab is almost here, and nobody's sure it should be. The two-seater robotaxi, no steering wheel, no pedals, exists as proof that Elon Musk genuinely believes autonomous driving has moved past the point where human controls are necessary. The company is planning a public launch soon, but the gap between Musk's confidence and what the vehicle has actually demonstrated in real-world conditions remains enormous.

The stakes are weird because Tesla isn't waiting for regulators to catch up. Full Self-Driving still racks up accidents and requires constant driver attention in ways that directly contradict what a steering-wheel-free car would demand. Pushing a vehicle with zero manual overrides onto public roads before the system handling it has proven itself bulletproof isn't bold, it's a bet that the early failures will be someone else's problem. The Cybercab exists because Musk said it would exist, not because the underlying technology has validated that it's safe.

What makes this launch moment actually interesting isn't the car itself. It's the question of whether regulators will let Tesla treat public roads as a beta testing ground for a fully autonomous vehicle when the company's own autonomous software still needs humans ready to take over constantly. If they do, Cybercab becomes the most expensive proof of concept in automotive history. If they don't, Musk's timeline collapses all over again, and we're back to waiting for technology to catch up to ambition.
