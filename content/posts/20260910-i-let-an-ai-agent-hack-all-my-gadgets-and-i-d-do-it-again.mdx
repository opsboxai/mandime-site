Will Knight handed an unrestricted AI model the keys to his entire home network and watched it systematically find his weak points. The model discovered vulnerabilities in his household devices, broke into a PC, and exposed exactly how exposed most people's digital lives actually are. But here's the part that matters: it also showed him how to fix it.

This isn't a disaster story dressed up as a thriller. Knight removed the safety guardrails from a powerful open-source model specifically to see what would happen, and the results are more useful than scary. An AI trained to find security holes without the usual "please don't break things" limitations turns out to be genuinely good at its job. It's the kind of ruthless vulnerability testing that security teams pay thousands for, except Knight got it running on his own hardware.

The real tension isn't whether the AI could break in. It's that most of us never test our defenses at all. Your router probably hasn't been updated in two years. You're reusing passwords across services. Your smart devices are phoning home to manufacturers you've never heard of. An AI doesn't need creativity to exploit these gaps—it just needs to look, and looking is what these models do best. The difference between getting hacked by a criminal and getting hacked by your own security test is the knowledge you walk away with.

Knight's experiment raises a practical question that's more valuable than the obvious one. Forget whether AI can be dangerous in the wrong hands. The question is whether deliberately pointing one at your own vulnerabilities, then actually fixing what it finds, isn't maybe the most useful thing most people could do right now. He did it so you'd know it's possible.
