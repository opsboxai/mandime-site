Right now, across most of the country, spiders are everywhere on the trail—and that's actually the entire point. They're not hunting you. They're hunting the bugs that would actually ruin your hike: mosquitoes, gnats, midges, the small flying things that turn a perfect morning into an itch fest. A spider in its web is doing free pest control, and understanding that changes how you move through the woods.

The visibility spike happens because spiders are building and rebuilding their webs constantly this time of year, especially orb weavers stringing their geometric traps between trees. You'll walk into a few. It's inevitable. That's not an attack, it's bad luck for the spider. They're not aggressive. They have no interest in biting you. The real statistics are embarrassingly one-sided: spider bites in the US are rare enough that they're barely tracked, and the ones that happen are almost always from a spider trapped against your skin—someone rolling over on it in bed, not a hiker on a trail. Fear and reality don't track here at all.

What actually matters is treating that encounter like the minor annoyance it is. Walk slowly through heavy vegetation. Watch where you're stepping in shelters. If you do hit a web, brush it off and move on. The spider will rebuild it. You'll be fine. Every mosquito that didn't bite you because a spider got there first is a small win worth respecting, even if spiders creep you out. The woods aren't being invaded. They're working exactly as designed.
