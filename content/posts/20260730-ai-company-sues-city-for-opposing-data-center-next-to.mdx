An AI company is suing a city for blocking a data center project next to a national park, and they're not alone. This is becoming the move when local governments say no to these massive facilities. The legal strategy is simple: bypass the people who live there, sue for approval anyway.

The tension makes sense from both angles. AI companies need enormous amounts of power and cooling to run their models, and they're willing to build near water sources and cheap electricity. Local governments see the environmental risk, the strain on infrastructure, and the permanent transformation of their region. But here's what's getting ugly: instead of negotiating or compromising, some operators are going straight to court. They're treating community opposition like a regulatory obstacle to litigate around rather than concerns to address.

This matters because it sets a precedent. If litigation wins where public process fails, every town near a good power source becomes a potential battleground. Water rights get tangled up, environmental reviews get challenged, and the people most affected have zero say in whether their backyard becomes a server farm. The AI boom is real and it needs infrastructure. But forcing it on communities that don't want it isn't innovation, it's just using lawyers instead of listening.
