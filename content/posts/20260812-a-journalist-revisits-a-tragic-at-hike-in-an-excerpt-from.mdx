Earl Swift spent decades carrying a detail most people would've buried. He'd hiked the Appalachian Trail years before, crossed paths with a young couple named Geoff and Molly in the spring of 1981, traded a few words with them on the trail, then moved on with his life. Two weeks later, both were dead. A drifter named Paul Bigley killed them near Franklin, North Carolina, and the randomness of it—that Swift had been exactly where they were, just days before it all went wrong—never really left him.

Now Swift has returned to the trail and written about it, digging into the case decades later, trying to make sense of the thin line between being in the right place and being in the wrong one at exactly the wrong moment. He wasn't responsible. He wasn't even there when it happened. But the fact that he could've been, that he was that close to witnessing something terrible or becoming part of it himself, is the kind of thing that sticks. It's the opposite of a near-miss. It's the recognition that survival on a long trail has more to do with luck than skill, that you can do everything right and still end up in the path of someone else's violence.

What makes the story worth revisiting isn't morbid fascination. It's that Swift is asking something harder: what does it mean to share a place with strangers, to be briefly connected by geography and circumstance, and then to learn that the random walk you both took down the same mountain could've ended completely differently. The Appalachian Trail is supposed to be a refuge, a place where a couple can walk away from the world and feel safe. But it's still the world. And sometimes bad luck wins.
