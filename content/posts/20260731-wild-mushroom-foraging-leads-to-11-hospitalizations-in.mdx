Eleven people ended up in the hospital this week in Montana after a foraging trip went sideways, and the culprit wasn't a single bad meal or contaminated batch. A group ate wild mushrooms they'd collected themselves, and someone in that group either didn't know what they were picking or made a call that looked safe and wasn't. This isn't the first time this has happened, and it won't be the last.

The thing about wild mushroom foraging is it looks deceptively simple online. You see the Instagram photos of baskets overflowing with chanterelles and porcini, the clean recipe posts, the "beginner's guide" that makes you think a field guide and an afternoon is enough. It's not. Toxic mushrooms often look nearly identical to edible ones. The difference between a dinner and an ER visit can be millimeters of gill structure, or a smell you have to know to recognize, or a time of year you picked it. Most people who forage seriously have spent years learning. They still second-guess themselves on borderline specimens.

What makes this particular incident worth paying attention to is scale. Eleven hospitalizations means one person didn't just eat a mushroom they weren't sure about. It means a whole group trusted the identification and got hurt for it. That's the real danger of foraging culture going mainstream without the apprenticeship it demands. Pick for yourself after you've spent a season learning on someone else's dime? Fine. Bring a haul to a potluck because you read a blog post? That's how you end up making eleven phone calls to poison control.
