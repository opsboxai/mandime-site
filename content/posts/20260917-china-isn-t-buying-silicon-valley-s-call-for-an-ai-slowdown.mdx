When Silicon Valley started asking governments to pump the brakes on AI development, they probably weren't thinking through what that actually meant geopolitically. Louise Matsakis lays out the core tension: the US and China both acknowledge that advanced AI systems pose real risks, but they're operating from completely different threat models.

Here's the thing that makes this interesting. A slowdown proposal that sounds reasonable to OpenAI or Anthropic looks to Beijing like a mechanism to cement American tech dominance. China's been sprinting to close the gap in AI capability for years. Telling them to slow down now, after the US already built the fastest models and the most sophisticated infrastructure, reads as a move to lock in that advantage. It's not paranoia. It's basic competition logic.

The US framing around AI safety is legitimate. There are real risks worth discussing. But framing it as a universal "we should all slow down" ignores that different countries have different starting positions. A slowdown hurts the player who's ahead less than the player who's chasing. Beijing gets that. They've watched this playbook before with semiconductors, where export controls and chip restrictions became the actual tool of competition. Asking for a slowdown after you've already built the lead is a different proposition than proposing it when everyone's neck and neck.

This matters because it means the voluntary self-regulation pitch that's been floating around Silicon Valley boardrooms probably isn't going to fly internationally. If the AI safety conversation stays framed as "responsible companies restraining themselves," it's going to get read as a western competitive move, not as genuine risk mitigation. That's not a bug in China's thinking. That's them reading the situation correctly.
