Shark sightings are up along U.S. coasts, and the footage is everywhere now. Drones have turned what used to be rare glimpses into constant visual confirmation. The thing is, increased sightings don't necessarily mean increased danger. They mostly mean better surveillance. A drone hovering over a packed beach in the Hamptons will catch a shark that, fifty years ago, would have swum past unnoticed and unreported. The sharks have always been there. We're just watching more carefully.

The real story underneath this is ecological. Sharks are moving into new waters because the conditions that pushed them out are changing. Water temperatures are shifting, prey populations are rebounding in coastal areas, and regulations have actually worked. Some shark species that were hammered by overfishing are coming back. That's not a sign the ocean is broken. That's a sign it's healing in ways we didn't necessarily plan for. It means sharing the water in ways humans haven't had to think about in decades.

For actual beachgoers, the math is straightforward and boring. You're statistically safer in the ocean than you are driving to the beach. Shark attacks in the U.S. average around 30 to 40 a year out of roughly 330 million people. Fatalities are rarer. The Atlantic coast sees the most activity, and even there, your odds of an encounter are vanishingly small. What you should actually worry about is rip currents, dehydration, and the sun. Those kill orders of magnitude more people annually. The shark is the headline. The water conditions are what matter.

If you're genuinely concerned, the old rules still work. Don't swim alone, avoid dawn and dusk when visibility is worst, stay out of the water if you're bleeding, and use common sense around fishing activity and seal colonies. Sharks aren't hunting you. They're doing what they've always done. Your job is just to be aware that they're there.
