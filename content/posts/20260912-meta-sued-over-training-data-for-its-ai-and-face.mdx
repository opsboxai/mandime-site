Meta is getting hit with a class action lawsuit over something a lot of people suspected but couldn't prove: the company systematically scraped billions of photos from Facebook and Instagram to train its AI models without asking permission or offering compensation.

The suit centers on two specific programs. First, there's the image-generation AI that Meta has been building and testing. Second is a face-recognition feature called "NameTag" that never actually shipped to the public, allegedly designed to identify people in photos at scale. Both tools relied on training data harvested directly from user uploads, according to the complaint. The scale here matters. We're talking about billions of images pulled from people's personal accounts, many of whom had no idea their photos were being fed into machine learning pipelines.

What makes this legally thorny is the timing and the terms. Meta's privacy policy technically covers a lot of ground, but there's a difference between "we might use your data for research" in a dense legal document and actually building a commercial face-recognition system from your family photos without explicit consent. The distinction between what users reasonably expected and what the company was actually doing is exactly where these lawsuits tend to find traction. This isn't the first time Meta has faced this kind of challenge, but it's one of the sharpest direct allegations about using personal photos as unlabeled training data for specific, unreleased products.

The fact that NameTag never launched complicates things slightly. Meta can argue it was experimental, not commercial. But the class action frames that differently: the company built the technology anyway, using peoples' data without permission, regardless of whether it ever went live. That's the argument that tends to survive early dismissal.
