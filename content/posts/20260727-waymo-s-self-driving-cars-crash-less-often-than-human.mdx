Waymo's robotaxis are crashing at roughly half the rate of human drivers in San Francisco, according to new data that actually matters. A study comparing real-world collision rates found the company's fully autonomous vehicles averaging around 2.1 crashes per million miles, compared to 4.1 for human drivers in the same city. That's not theoretical. That's miles actually driven on real streets with real traffic.

Here's what makes this different from the usual self-driving hype cycle. The research comes from people studying actual crash reports and insurance data, not Waymo's own marketing. The robotaxis are operating in one of the most congested, unpredictable urban environments in the country, which means they're not cherry-picked test scenarios. They're dealing with aggressive lane changes, pedestrians ignoring signals, and every other variable that makes city driving hard.

But there's a catch worth understanding. This doesn't mean the technology is done. The researchers themselves flagged that more data collection is essential before drawing any permanent conclusions. Waymo's fleet is still relatively small compared to the millions of human drivers, so statistical flukes can matter more. One bad quarter or a cluster of incidents could shift the numbers. And there's a selection effect worth mentioning: Waymo's cars mostly operate on routes they've mapped extensively and in conditions they've been trained for, while human drivers go everywhere in all weather at 3am after drinks.

What it does signal is that the gap between the hype and the actual capability is finally shrinking. For years, self-driving companies promised everything and delivered mostly videos. Now they're producing collision rates that can be measured against a real baseline. That's progress you can actually point to.
