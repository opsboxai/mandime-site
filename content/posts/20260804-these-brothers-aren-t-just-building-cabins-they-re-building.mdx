Benito and Arjuna Steen grew up watching their parents build a house out of straw and clay. Not as a novelty or a back-to-the-land experiment that lasted one season. As actual kids, they were part of the work. They learned early that a structure is something you understand because you built it, that craftsmanship is inherited the way DNA is, and that there's a difference between owning a house and knowing one.

Now they're master carpenters doing the same thing their kids are watching them do. Building cabins. Real ones, the kind people pay for and live in. The cycle isn't romantic or ironic or curated for Instagram. It's just what happens when you teach someone to build instead of telling them to buy, when you show them that their hands matter more than their wallet, and when you do that work in front of them long enough that it becomes normal. Unremarkable. Part of who they are.

There's something lost in most American childhoods now. Not just the skill of building, but the deeper thing underneath it: the idea that you can make something real and substantial, that your effort produces something that outlasts you, that you have that kind of power. The Steen brothers' kids have that. They watched their fathers build. They'll build for their own kids. The cabin becomes the classroom, the sawdust becomes the curriculum, and the tradition becomes the inheritance that actually matters.
