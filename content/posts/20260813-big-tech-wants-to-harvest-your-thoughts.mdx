Silicon Valley is literally trying to read your mind, and most people have no idea this is happening right now. Companies like Neuralink, Meta, and a dozen others you've never heard of are already developing consumer neurotechnology products that can monitor your brain activity in real time. We're not talking about sci-fi dystopia here. We're talking about headsets and implants in various stages of development that will, if they hit the market, create an entirely new category of personal data: your thoughts.

The privacy implications are genuinely wild. Your location data, your browsing history, your messages—those things at least require you to interact with a device. Your thoughts don't. A neural interface that can decode what's happening in your brain could theoretically capture intention, desire, memory, emotional state, and cognitive patterns without you doing a single thing. And once that data exists, it becomes valuable. Advertising networks want it. Insurance companies want it. Employers want it. The infrastructure for exploitation doesn't even need to be built yet because the data collection is already underway.

What makes this different from previous privacy invasions is that there's no off switch you control. You can't leave your phone at home or delete your browser history. Your brain is with you constantly. The regulatory framework for neurotech privacy is essentially nonexistent. There's no FTC rule, no state law, nothing concrete stopping companies from building products that harvest neural data and monetizing it the same way they've monetized every other piece of your digital life. James Crawford's work here serves as an early alarm bell for what's coming if the tech industry is allowed to move at its usual speed and the government continues to move at its usual glacial pace.
