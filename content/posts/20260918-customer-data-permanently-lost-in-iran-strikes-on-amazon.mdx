Amazon just admitted something most cloud companies hope never comes up in public: some of your data is gone, and they can't get it back. When Iran's ballistic missiles hit data centers in the Middle East, AWS discovered that their backup systems and redundancy protocols, designed for earthquakes and power failures, didn't account for a direct military strike. The company has told customers whose data was stored in affected regions that recovery isn't possible.

This matters because AWS doesn't just host some startups and side projects. They run infrastructure for hospitals, financial services, government agencies, and basically anything that depends on the cloud staying up. The implicit promise of cloud computing is that your data lives in multiple places simultaneously, safe from the kind of disasters that would wreck a single physical server room. Except nobody really designed around "direct hit from a state actor's weapons."

The striking part isn't that data can be lost in an extreme scenario. The striking part is that AWS apparently didn't war-game this one, despite running critical infrastructure in geopolitically volatile regions. Backup systems get replicated across data centers in different locations. But if those locations all get hit in the same strike, that redundancy becomes theoretical. AWS now faces the question every infrastructure company dreads: how do you design for something that sounds paranoid until it actually happens?
