An avalanche on Broad Peak, the twelfth-highest mountain in the world at 26,400 feet, has killed multiple climbers and triggered one of the largest rescue operations in the Karakoram range. Search parties are now combing the slope, and nearby K2 expeditions—climbers on one of Earth's deadliest mountains—have suspended their own attempts to help with recovery. That level of coordination tells you how serious this is: K2 climbers are voluntarily stepping back, which almost never happens at that altitude and cost.

The Broad Peak disaster underscores something climbers know but the rest of us forget: high-altitude mountaineering kills you in ways that rescue teams can't always prevent. You're above the cloud deck, you're operating on bottled oxygen or borrowed air from your own blood, and avalanche risk is part of the terrain. Rescue helicopters can't reliably hover above 23,000 feet. Ground teams have to move on foot through the same unstable snow that triggered the slide. By the time search parties arrive, the conditions that killed climbers are still waiting.

The climbers on Broad Peak were on a route that's become more trafficked in recent years as expeditions seek alternatives to the more famous peaks. That doesn't make it safer. It makes it busier. More fixed ropes, more foot traffic destabilizing the snowpack, more people on the mountain when the mountain decides to move. Recovery operations will take days, possibly weeks. For the families of those missing, the waiting is the hardest part. For the rescue teams, the work ahead is both necessary and brutal.
