An early aughts news anchor's hair isn't just a style choice, it's a psychological weapon. That particular blend of volume, texture, and calculated casualness was designed to project authority while seeming relatable. Tony Ward knew this going in when he started building Robert Pattinson's look for "Primetime," and he wasn't starting from scratch with some vague '90s nostalgia brief. He was chasing something specific: the exact visual language that made those true crime personalities feel trustworthy enough to deliver stories about other people's worst moments.

The key was borrowing directly from what actually worked in broadcast television during that era. TV hair had rules. You needed movement without looking styled, body without looking blown out, and a texture that read on camera but felt natural in person. Ward used those same principles that made anchors work in HD to make Pattinson's character work as a fictional version of that same archetype. The hair had to make you believe this guy spent his days with a earpiece and a teleprompter, even if you knew nothing else about the movie.

That's the difference between costume and character. Anyone can slap a haircut from a decade on an actor and call it period-accurate. The real work is understanding why that specific style existed in the first place, what job it was supposed to do, and then weaponizing it to make the performance work harder.
