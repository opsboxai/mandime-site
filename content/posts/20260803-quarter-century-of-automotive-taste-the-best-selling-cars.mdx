The Honda Civic spent most of the 1980s and 90s as America's best-selling car, which tells you everything you need to know about what happened to Detroit during those decades. While American manufacturers were still pushing full-size sedans and trucks, Honda figured out that people actually wanted something efficient, reliable, and fun to drive, and they made a killing on it. The Civic wasn't the fastest or the fanciest, but it worked. It showed up. It didn't break down on you.

By the time the Toyota Camry took over in the 2000s, the shift was basically complete. Buyers had learned their lesson about American iron and weren't going back. The Camry represented the final victory of the Japanese playbook: solid engineering, boring predictability, and a lifespan that would outlast most marriages. It wasn't aspirational. Nobody dreamed about owning a Camry. But everyone understood why you'd buy one, and that's worth more than dream.

What's wild is how fast the full-size American sedan disappeared from the conversation. For decades, that was the default car, the thing you got when you wanted to be a normal adult with a family. Then suddenly it wasn't. The market didn't just shift. It wholesale rejected what Detroit had been selling for fifty years and never looked back. The best-selling cars of that era weren't revolutionary or even particularly interesting. They were just competent when competence was rare, and that was enough to own a generation's worth of driveways.
