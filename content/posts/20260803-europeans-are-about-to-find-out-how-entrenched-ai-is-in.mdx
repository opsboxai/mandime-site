The European Union just mandated transparency on something most people don't even realize is already running their lives. Starting now, companies have to tell you when you're talking to AI, when you're looking at deepfakes or AI-generated images, when algorithms are making decisions about you. Sounds reasonable. The catch is the volume. Banks use AI to approve your mortgage. Dating apps use it to rank who shows up in your feed. Your email filters out spam with it. Your maps app predicts traffic with it. Retailers decide what products to show you with it. So now what, every time you open Gmail you get a popup saying "AI is sorting this"? Every swipe on a dating app comes with a disclosure?

That's the actual problem the EU accidentally created. They're right that people should know when machines are steering their choices. But slap a thousand disclosures on everything and people tune them out entirely. It's the same reason nobody reads terms of service. The solution becomes white noise. You get disclosure fatigue before you get any actual informed consent. The person who designed the rule probably imagined you reading each one carefully, thinking through the implications. The person using the app will just tap "OK" and move on.

The real tension isn't between privacy and convenience anymore. It's between transparency that matters and transparency that exhausts. The EU forced the conversation without solving how to make it work at actual human scale. Now companies are scrambling to figure out how to comply without making their products unusable, and users are about to learn just how much of their day is already algorithmic. That part's probably worth knowing. The disclosure? That's where it gets messy.
