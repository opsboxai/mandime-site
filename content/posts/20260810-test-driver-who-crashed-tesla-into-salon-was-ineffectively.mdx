A test driver in a Tesla Model 3 plowed straight through the front window of a hair salon in California, and now the lawsuit is zeroing in on something you'd think would be basic: the guy had no idea how to actually drive the thing.

Here's where it gets interesting. The driver had never owned an EV before, never test-driven one, and apparently got minimal instruction on how regenerative braking works, where the one-pedal driving mode lives, or what happens when you confuse the accelerator for the brake. He hit the gas instead of the brake, crashed through the salon window, and injured people inside. Standard nightmare scenario. But the lawsuit isn't just naming Tesla as the defendant. It's arguing that whoever handed him the keys failed to prep him for something that handles fundamentally different from a gas car, especially in an emergency.

That's the trap with EVs right now. They're marketed as intuitive. Press the pedal, go forward. But the actual driving dynamics are weird if you're used to a combustion engine. Regen braking means you're slowing down without touching the brake pedal. The weight distribution is totally different. The throttle response is immediate. A gas car gives you a split second of mechanical delay before things happen. An EV doesn't. If you don't know that going in, and someone hands you the keys for a test drive with a five-minute YouTube explanation and a thumbs up, you're basically driving blind.

The real question isn't whether Tesla makes a good car. It's whether putting someone behind the wheel of something this behaviorally different without actual hands-on instruction is negligence. The courts will decide where that line is, but if you're ever test-driving an EV for the first time, you now know what to ask for: an actual explanation of how it drives, not just a "go have fun" and a wave goodbye.
