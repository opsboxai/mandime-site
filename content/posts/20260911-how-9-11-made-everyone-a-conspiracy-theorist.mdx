Twenty-three years later, we're still living in the wreckage of what 9/11 did to how Americans think. Not the buildings. The collective paranoia.

Before September 11, conspiracy theories were fringe material. Shortwave radio stuff. The kind of thing your weird uncle believed, not your neighbor, your coworker, your mom. Then the towers fell and suddenly the official story wasn't enough for millions of people. The government had missed it. The government had to be lying about something. And once you start down that road, you don't stop at "maybe they had a security failure." You go deeper. Someone had to have known. Someone had to have profited. Someone had to be hiding something. And if they were hiding that, what else?

The internet was ready. Forums and early blogs gave conspiracy theories a distribution network that shortwave radio could never dream of. YouTube wouldn't exist for another four years, but when it did, it became the perfect tool for tying disparate pieces of footage together and narrating them into a coherent (if wildly unfounded) alternative history. Alex Jones didn't invent the conspiracy machine. 9/11 broke it open and handed him the megaphone.

What's genuinely unsettling is how normal it became. A guy in 2001 peddling wild theories about government involvement looked crazy. A guy in 2005 looked like he was asking reasonable questions. By 2010 he had a major media platform. The Overton window had shifted so far that what was once unthinkable became mainstream talk show material. We didn't just get better at spreading lies. We got better at believing them. And we stopped even pretending to care about evidence the way we used to.

The damage wasn't to one building or 3,000 lives, as horrible as that was. It was to the basic national agreement about what's true. And we haven't recovered from that yet.
