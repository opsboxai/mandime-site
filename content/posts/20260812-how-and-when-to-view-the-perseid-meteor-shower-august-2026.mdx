The Perseids are about to put on their best show in years, and 2026 is the year to actually get out and watch it. Peak viewing lands August 12 and 13, but here's the thing that makes this one special: the moon will be nearly new, which means minimal light pollution washing out the faint streaks. That's the difference between catching a few dozen meteors per hour and actually seeing the full chaos unfold across the sky.

The radiant point sits in Perseus, which climbs highest in the northern sky around midnight and stays visible until dawn. You don't need a telescope or fancy equipment. Find somewhere dark, away from city lights, lie back, and let your eyes adjust for twenty minutes. Rural areas, state parks, or anywhere you can actually see the Milky Way will work. Beaches and mountains are ideal, but even a dark backyard beats trying to watch from town.

What makes the Perseid shower so reliable is its source: debris left behind by Comet Swift-Tuttle as it orbits the sun. Every August, Earth plows through that debris stream, and the particles burn up in the atmosphere at around 133,000 mph. The result is consistent, predictable, and genuinely worth the planning. Even if clouds roll in on peak night, the show extends across several days on either side, so you've got flexibility. Start watching after midnight, when the radiant is highest, and stick with it until predawn. This isn't a quick peek. It's worth staying up for.
