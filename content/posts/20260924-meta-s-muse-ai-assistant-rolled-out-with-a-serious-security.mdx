Meta's new Muse AI assistant shipped with a zero-day vulnerability that would have given attackers complete control over a victim's Mac. Dan Goodin at Ars Technica reported that the flaw was serious enough that an intruder could execute arbitrary commands, access files, steal credentials, and essentially do whatever they wanted on the infected machine. The good news: Meta caught it and patched it before widespread exploitation. The bad news: this is exactly the kind of risk that comes with rushing AI agents into the wild.

Muse is designed to handle tasks on your computer by interacting with the UI the same way a human would. That's powerful and genuinely useful, but it also means the assistant needs deep system access to work. When that access isn't properly gated, you end up with something that looks like a feature but functions as a remote control for your entire machine. A malicious prompt or a compromised website could have weaponized it instantly.

This isn't a unique problem to Meta. Any AI agent that touches your system files, runs terminal commands, or manages credentials is a potential attack surface. As these tools proliferate, the gap between capability and safety keeps getting exposed. Meta handled this one correctly by fixing it fast, but it's a reminder that the convenience of an AI that can do whatever you ask comes with a cost. The question is whether companies are building security into these tools from the start, or just patching holes as they blow up.
