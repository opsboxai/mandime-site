A cancer that spreads between fish like an infection just showed up in catfish populations spanning across the US and Canadian border. This isn't some theoretical lab scenario either. Scientists actually found it in wild catfish in a shared lake system, and it's the first documented case of a transmissible cancer in North American freshwater fish.

Here's what makes this genuinely strange: most cancers don't jump between individuals. They're locked into one body. But this one travels. It's similar to contagious cancers found in other species like Tasmanian devils and certain clams, but finding it in catfish in a working lake system is new territory. The cancer appears to spread through direct contact, which in a contained freshwater population means it could theoretically reach anything sharing that water. The lake doesn't know about state borders.

The real question isn't whether this is a disaster right now. It's whether this is a warning sign about what we're missing. If a transmissible cancer can establish itself in a major freshwater system without us knowing about it until researchers were specifically looking, how many other things are happening in lakes and rivers that we're completely unaware of? The catfish aren't going anywhere. But the fact that we found this one almost by accident suggests we should probably be looking harder at what else is quietly spreading through the water we thought we understood.
