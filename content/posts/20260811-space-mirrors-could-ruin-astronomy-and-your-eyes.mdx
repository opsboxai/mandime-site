A Russian experiment in 1992 bounced sunlight off a mirror in orbit down to Earth. It worked. That should terrify everyone who actually looks through a telescope.

The concept sounds almost quaint: giant reflective satellites parked in space, angling sunlight onto dark regions of the planet to extend daylight or power solar farms around the clock. It's been kicking around since the 1920s. The Russians called theirs Znamya. But here's what nobody really talks about. A mirror the size of a city orbiting overhead doesn't just illuminate dark valleys. It creates a second sun. And unlike the actual sun, which you at least know to avoid looking at, a space mirror could appear anywhere, anytime, with no warning.

Astronomers are already sounding the alarm. Ground-based telescopes operating at night would get absolutely hammered. Satellites tracking distant galaxies, measuring cosmic expansion, hunting for exoplanets—all of it gets flooded out by artificial glare brighter than moonlight. The research that depends on pristine night skies doesn't recover from that. It just stops.

But the eye damage angle is the one that cuts different. A concentrated beam of reflected sunlight can burn through a retina faster than your pupils can react. We're not talking about staring at the sun. We're talking about a bright spot appearing in the sky while you're looking that direction, with no way to predict it, no eclipse glasses helping, no natural instinct to look away fast enough. The damage happens in milliseconds.

The people pushing these projects talk about economic benefits and energy abundance. They're not wrong that the problem is real. But they're treating it like a physics problem when it's actually a systems problem. Once one country launches a solar mirror satellite, everyone wants one. You can't unsee the value. And the moment the sky becomes a competitive resource—something you can own and weaponize and beam wherever you want—astronomy as we know it becomes a luxury hobby for rich people with underground observatories. That's not a future worth accelerating toward.
