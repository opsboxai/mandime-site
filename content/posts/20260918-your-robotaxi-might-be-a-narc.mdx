The moment you step into a driverless Waymo, you're not just hiring a ride. You're getting into a vehicle equipped with multiple cameras, microphones, and sensors that can detect weapons, aggressive behavior, and anything else the company decides violates its terms of service. In early September, two teenagers in Los Angeles learned this the hard way when Waymo detected what it flagged as a firearm violation, remotely pulled the car over, and called the cops on them before they'd even reached their destination. Police showed up and made arrests based on evidence gathered inside a private vehicle that the passengers thought was just a taxi.

This isn't some theoretical privacy concern buried in fine print. Waymo has built a surveillance infrastructure that operates in real time, with the company acting as both witness and judge. They decide what constitutes a violation, they decide when to alert authorities, and they do it all without a warrant, without a courtroom, without the passengers knowing they're being watched by more than just a driver. The cameras in these cars are there ostensibly for safety and liability. In practice, they're also there to monitor you, flag your behavior, and hand that footage to law enforcement.

The teenagers in the LA case were carrying a BB gun, not an actual firearm, according to reporting. That distinction apparently didn't matter much to Waymo's detection system or the cops who showed up. What matters here is the precedent. Robotaxis operate in public space but feel private. They're becoming another layer of ambient surveillance in a city that already has more cameras watching its citizens than most people realize. And unlike a police officer pulling you over, you can't even see the witness testifying against you. It's happening in the backend of an algorithm, on a server somewhere, before you've done anything wrong in any legal sense.
