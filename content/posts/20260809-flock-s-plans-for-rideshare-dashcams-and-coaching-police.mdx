Flock, the surveillance startup that's already embedded itself into police departments across the country, is quietly expanding into rideshare cars and officer training. The company's pitch to Uber and Lyft drivers is straightforward: install a dashcam that flags suspicious behavior, then sell that footage back to cops. It's the logical next step for a company that's made its name turning every dashcam into a potential evidence locker for law enforcement.

What makes this move notable isn't the technology itself, it's the scope. Flock already operates the largest network of private surveillance cameras in the US, pulling footage from traffic cameras, parking lots, and doorbell networks to help police investigate crimes. Adding rideshare vehicles doesn't just expand their reach into another slice of urban surveillance. It creates a system where regular people become unpaid information collectors for police departments, all while the footage gets stored on private servers that law enforcement can query. The company is also moving into police training, which amounts to coaching officers on how to use their platform more effectively.

The rideshare angle is where things get weird. Flock's system would flag "suspicious behavior" in cars, but what counts as suspicious? Fighting? Drug use? Or just an argument between friends that an algorithm decides looks wrong? The company hasn't been transparent about its criteria, and Uber and Lyft drivers signing up probably won't know exactly what their cameras are trained to detect. They'll install a device, get paid, and contribute to a surveillance infrastructure they don't fully understand.

The police coaching piece is darker still. When a startup isn't just selling tools to law enforcement but actively training officers how to use them, you've crossed from vendor into partner. Flock gets to shape how police investigate cases, which investigations get resources, and what kinds of footage cops think to request. That's influence over policing strategy, not just technology.
