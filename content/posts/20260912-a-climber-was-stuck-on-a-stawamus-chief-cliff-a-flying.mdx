A climber got stuck halfway up Stawamus Chief in Squamish, British Columbia at dusk with no jacket and no way down. Instead of waiting for a traditional ground rescue, a drone team flew a rope and warm layer directly to him on the cliff face. It worked. The climber used the delivered rope to rappel to safety.

This isn't a stunt or proof of concept. This was a real problem solved in real time on one of Canada's most visited climbing destinations. The drones did what ground teams couldn't do fast: deliver gear vertically to a person who was genuinely exposed and stranded. A light drone lit up the cliff so operators could see what they were doing. A cargo drone carried the actual supplies. They threaded it between rock features and weather while managing payload weight and battery life in the dark.

What makes this stick is the simplicity of it. The guy needed a rope and a jacket. Those exact things showed up. No complicated technical rescue, no helicopter, no rappelling ground team taking hours to reach him. It's a clean use case for technology that's mostly spent hauling packages across suburbia or doing aerial shots for wedding videos. Rescue teams in technical terrain have started training with drones specifically for this: reaching people in places ground crews can't access quickly or safely. The Stawamus Chief rescue shows it's past the theoretical stage. When a climber gets stuck at 2000 feet on a granite face at night, a drone can actually change the outcome.
