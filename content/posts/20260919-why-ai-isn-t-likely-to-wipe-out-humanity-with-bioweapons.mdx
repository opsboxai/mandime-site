The bioweapon apocalypse scenario gets all the headlines, but actual biosecurity researchers think you're worrying about the wrong doomsday. Kate Knibbs and Emily Mullin dug into why AI-designed pathogens, while theoretically possible, rank surprisingly low on the list of credible extinction-level threats from advanced AI systems.

The math doesn't work the way Hollywood imagines it. Creating a genuinely catastrophic bioweapon requires solving multiple hard problems simultaneously: designing a pathogen that's both transmissible enough to spread globally and lethal enough to matter, then actually manufacturing it at scale. AI might help with one piece of that puzzle, but it can't magic away the wet lab reality. You still need actual biology to happen in the actual world. A model trained on genetic sequences can suggest modifications, but suggesting and executing are galaxies apart. The real biosecurity people worry less about a superintelligent plague and more about the mundane risk: that better tools for pathogen research get cheaper and more accessible, giving a handful of determined people in a garage somewhere capabilities that used to require a nation-state.

The bigger AI risks, by contrast, are often the quieter ones. Economic disruption, labor displacement, decision-making systems that encode bias at scale, cyberattacks that don't need to kill you to cause real damage. The existential threat worth thinking about isn't the one that requires a perfectly executed doomsday scenario. It's the one that compounds, spreads, and becomes embedded before anyone agrees there's a problem.
