The nonprofit world is basically preparing for a wealth transfer that hasn't happened yet. When Anthropic and OpenAI eventually go public, their employees will suddenly have liquid equity worth serious money, and nonprofits are already positioning themselves to catch that wave. It's not cynical so much as it is practical: the last time this happened at scale was the dot-com boom, and before that, the early days of Facebook and Google. The math is simple. Anthropic has somewhere north of 500 employees. OpenAI has roughly 1,500. If either company reaches a $100 billion valuation at IPO (plausible for OpenAI, possible for Anthropic given the pace of AI investment), early employees could walk away with eight or nine figures. That kind of instant wealth tends to produce sudden philanthropists.

What's interesting is that nonprofits aren't just hoping this happens. They're actively preparing infrastructure to capture donations. Some are hiring fundraisers specifically trained to approach newly liquid tech workers. Others are building pitch decks tailored to the AI ethics crowd, which both companies employ in volume. The calculation is that first-time major donors often give within months of a liquidity event, usually to causes that align with their identity or values. Someone who spent five years arguing for AI safety inside OpenAI is probably going to want to fund AI safety research. Someone who believed in Anthropic's constitutional AI thesis might fund alignment work or effective altruism projects. The nonprofits that move first and speak that language will win.

The wildcard is how many of these employees will actually sell versus hold. If the stock rallies hard post-IPO, some will sit on positions for years. Some will diversify aggressively. Some will spend it on houses in the Bay Area like everyone else. But enough of them will become visible, new philanthropists that the ecosystem is worth preparing for. And unlike most startup booms, this one carries ideological weight. These companies employ people who think deeply about the impact of what they're building. That tends to produce givers who actually care where their money goes, which is harder to pin down but ultimately more valuable to serious nonprofits than lottery windfalls from random tech bros.
