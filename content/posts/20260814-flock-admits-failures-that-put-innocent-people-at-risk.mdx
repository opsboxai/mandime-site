Flock, the ALPR company that powers thousands of police searches through camera networks at gas stations and parking lots, just admitted their system was getting innocent people pulled over. CEO Garrett Langley didn't soften it, either. He straight called it "a failed system" and said the company's data was being used in ways that put people at risk.

Here's what actually happened. Flock's technology reads license plates and flags matches against police databases. Sounds logical. But the company had almost no guardrails on how cops could search that data. A detective looking for a vehicle could cast a net so wide it would spit back plates that had nothing to do with the actual crime. Wrong car, wrong person, traffic stop anyway. In one case out of Arizona, a woman was stopped four times in a single week because her vehicle matched plates flagged in searches so broad they were essentially useless. She had nothing to do with any crime.

So Flock is changing course. They're implementing stricter search rules that limit how cops can query the system, cutting data retention periods, and requiring better justification before a search gets approved. It's damage control, sure, but it's the kind of admission and pivot that matters. The company had leverage and chose to tighten the screws on themselves instead of waiting for regulators or lawsuits to force it. Whether these changes actually work depends entirely on how Flock enforces them and how police departments adapt. But at least they stopped pretending the system was working fine.
