By 1997, the internet had convinced itself it needed a democratic way to rate human attractiveness. James Hong and Jim Young built Hot or Not, a site where you uploaded a photo and strangers scored you on a 1-to-10 scale. It was stupid and it worked. Within months, they were getting fifty thousand submissions a day. Within years, it had shaped how an entire generation thought about dating.

The wild part isn't that Hot or Not existed. It's that we never actually left. The mechanics Hot or Not pioneered, Megan Tomos writes in a piece for Wired, became the template for everything that came after. Tinder, Hinge, Bumble, every swipe-based app that dominates dating now is just Hot or Not with a better UI and venture capital. You're still ranking people at scale. You're still reducing attraction to a binary. The only difference is the app companies learned to dress it up in language about connection and compatibility. But the core transaction is identical to what Hong and Young figured out in 1997, locked in a basement with a webcam.

What's shifted is the stakes. Back then, Hot or Not was a novelty. Now it's how millions of people actually meet, fall in love, and form relationships. Dating apps have tried to escape the rating-factory culture the original site created, pushing algorithms and personality questions and video prompts instead of just asking you to swipe yes or no. But the swipe is too convenient, too addictive, and frankly too profitable for any of them to kill it off. So we're still playing the same game we were playing in the late 90s, just with better phones and worse odds of meeting someone genuinely interested in you rather than killing time on a commute. Hot or Not didn't just build a website. It built the internet we're still swiping through.
