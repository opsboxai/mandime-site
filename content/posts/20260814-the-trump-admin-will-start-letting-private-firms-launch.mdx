The Trump administration just gave private companies legal cover to launch cyberattacks on foreign targets. A presidential memorandum signed this week authorizes private firms to conduct offensive operations against criminal networks overseas, provided they're operating under federal government control and oversight. It's a significant shift: until now, cyberattacks have been the exclusive domain of government agencies like the NSA and Cyber Command. Now private contractors get a seat at the table.

The logic is straightforward. Foreign cybercriminals operate faster than government bureaucracy can respond. Private firms already work in this space anyway, monitoring threats and conducting defensive operations. By formalizing offensive capabilities under federal supervision, the administration argues it can move quicker and hit harder against ransomware gangs, state-sponsored hackers, and criminal syndicates that drain billions from American businesses and institutions every year. The memorandum includes oversight provisions, suggesting this isn't a blank check.

But this opens doors that are genuinely hard to close. Private firms answer to profit margins first. The line between authorized targets and collateral damage gets blurry fast. What counts as "under the control and oversight" of federal agencies? Who pays if something goes wrong? And once this legal framework exists, the pressure to expand it only grows. It's efficient until it isn't, and by then you've already normalized something that used to require an act of Congress to justify.
