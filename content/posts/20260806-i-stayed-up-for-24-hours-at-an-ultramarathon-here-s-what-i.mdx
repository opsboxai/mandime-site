Fred Dreier showed up to California's Western States Endurance Run as a non-runner with a straightforward question: why would someone voluntarily run 100 miles? He found the answer wasn't about toughness or some masochistic need to suffer. It was weirder and more human than that.

Ultramarathoners aren't chasing a finish time or a medal that matters to anyone but themselves. They're after something closer to clarity. Spend 24 hours moving through the Sierra Nevada mountains, and your brain does something different. The usual noise quiets down. You stop thinking about the job, the inbox, the endless scroll. Pain becomes simple and honest in a way that daily life never is. Crew members at the race told him they'd watched runners go through genuine transformation over a single night, not because they won anything, but because they proved something to themselves that couldn't be faked or outsourced.

The boom in ultrarunning isn't mysterious once you see it this way. People are desperate for experiences that mean something. A 100-miler demands everything you have. There's no coasting, no half-measures, nowhere to hide. For 24 hours, you're completely present. That's rare now. And it matters enough that thousands of people are willing to spend months training and race day pushing their bodies toward failure, just to have it.
