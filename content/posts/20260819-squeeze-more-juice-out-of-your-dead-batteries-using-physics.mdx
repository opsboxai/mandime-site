A dead battery isn't actually dead. It still holds voltage, just not enough to power most devices. That's where the joule thief comes in, a deceptively simple circuit that extracts the remaining energy a regular device would ignore, using nothing but an inductor, a transistor, a resistor, and a diode.

The trick is oscillation. The circuit rapidly switches on and off, charging an inductor coil with low voltage, then releasing that stored energy in a sharp spike. That spike can push the voltage high enough to actually do work, like powering an LED or a small radio. Rhett Allain breaks down the physics: you're not creating energy from nothing. You're converting a trickle of current into concentrated bursts, a clever hack that exploits the difference between average voltage and peak voltage. A battery that reads 0.7 volts might be useless in a flashlight, but a joule thief can squeeze another hour of light out of it.

It's the kind of thing that matters more in theory than in daily life. Most people don't need to resurrect their AAs. But the principle is elegant and it works, and there's something satisfying about understanding why a cheap circuit can do what seems impossible. The joule thief was popularized in the maker community about two decades ago, and it's still a solid intro to how inductors actually behave outside a textbook.
