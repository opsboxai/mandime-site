There's a moment when you hand a credit card and passwords to software and genuinely don't know if you're being smart or reckless. That's where Instinct lives right now.

The pitch is clean: an AI agent that handles the tedious stuff. Book your restaurants. Find deals. Spot scams before they land in your inbox. Zoë Schiffer tested it and the numbers are real. It saved $550 on purchases, actually completed reservation requests without requiring her to redo the work three times, and caught a phishing attempt that looked legitimate. Those aren't small wins. That's the difference between an AI tool and something that actually changes how you move through your day.

But here's the catch, and it matters: the same access that lets it save you money also means it's sitting on your email, your shopping history, and your financial patterns. Schiffer lost $64 on a flawed purchase decision the agent made without asking. That's the low end of bad. The high end is a service breach or a prompt injection attack that turns your AI assistant into a very efficient thief. The company hasn't published a full security audit. The business model isn't entirely clear. You're betting on trust in a category where trust is still being invented.

This is the real tension with agent software right now. The ones that are actually useful are the ones that scare you a little. Safe AI agents do nothing. Useful ones make you sign forms acknowledging you understand the risk. Schiffer's take is honest about that tradeoff instead of pretending one side doesn't exist, and that's worth reading if you're considering letting any software run your life in the background.
