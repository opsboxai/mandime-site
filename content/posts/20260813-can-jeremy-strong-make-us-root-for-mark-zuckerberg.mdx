Jeremy Strong's turn as Kendall Roy didn't just make you sympathize with a guy actively destroying his own life. It made you understand him. You watched a spoiled, medicated, terrified man sabotage everything because the alternative—admitting he wasn't special—felt worse than total collapse. That's the kind of acting that rewires how you see a character, the kind that earns a guy a real place in prestige television.

Now he's playing Mark Zuckerberg in The Social Reckoning, and the assignment is harder in ways that matter. Kendall was fiction. Zuckerberg is a real person who built a real platform that genuinely changed the world, and not always for good. The question isn't whether Strong can make you empathize with the guy. It's whether a story about Zuckerberg can be built in a way that doesn't ask you to do something ethically complicated just to follow the narrative. Strong's approach to character work is famous for being total and uncompromising. He doesn't phone it in. But there's a difference between understanding why a fictional failson makes terrible decisions and being guided toward understanding why the actual architect of Facebook made his.

The risk is real. Prestige drama has a way of humanizing power in ways that end up serving power. The best version of this film would be the one that doesn't trade in sympathy. It's the one that uses Strong's talent to show you exactly how a person convinced himself that moving fast and breaking things was worth what got broken. That's not the same as forgiving him for it.
