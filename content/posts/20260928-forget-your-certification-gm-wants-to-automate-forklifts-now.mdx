General Motors just filed a patent for a forklift that ditches the driver's seat entirely and replaces it with a coupling system that lets multiple units link up in sequence. This isn't some theoretical warehouse of the future—it's a concrete engineering solution to a real logistics problem: forklifts spend hours moving pallets one at a time, and half that labor is just the operator ferrying the machine back for another load.

The coupling mechanism transforms the dynamic. Instead of a single forklift making ten trips, you could theoretically chain three or four together, having one operator guide the whole train while the linked units follow autonomously. That's not just efficiency math on a spreadsheet. That's fewer jobs on a warehouse floor, faster throughput, and fewer collision risks from repeated back-and-forth movements. It also means no operator fatigue on repetitive routes through the same warehouse aisles, which is where most accidents actually happen.

What makes this angle sharper than the "robots are coming for your job" narrative is the specificity. GM isn't building a full autonomous warehouse system or a sci-fi sorting facility. They're solving one mechanical problem: how do you make a forklift do more work per operator input. The coupling design is elegant partly because it sidesteps the complexity of full AI navigation. A single human driver can manage the lead unit, and physics handles the rest.

The real question isn't whether this works. It's timing. Warehousing is already tight on labor, and adoption by major logistics operators could happen faster than people expect. Forklift operators have some runway, but not as much as they think.
