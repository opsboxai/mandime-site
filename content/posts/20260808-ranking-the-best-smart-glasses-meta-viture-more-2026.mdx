Smart glasses have quietly become the most practical wearable nobody's actually wearing yet. The gap between the hype and what's actually sitting on your face right now is enormous, and that's the real story here.

The current crop runs a specific spectrum. Meta's Ray-Bans lean hard into the camera and AI assistant angle, treating glasses like a portable documentation device paired with voice commands. Viture's pushing the opposite direction, designing around a tiny display that sits in your peripheral vision without blocking your actual sight line. Both solve different problems, which means neither solves the main one: they're still not something you'd grab instead of your phone on instinct.

The core limitation stays unchanged. Battery life maxes out around 8 hours for the heavy hitters. The display tech on the ones with actual screens is still grainy compared to what you're used to. Voice control works until you're in a coffee shop and don't want to be that guy. And the price point hasn't dropped enough to justify wearing a device that does less than the thing already in your pocket. That's not being cynical. That's just where we are in 2026.

What's actually worth paying attention to is the trajectory. These aren't toys anymore. They're just not quite there yet. If you're already thinking about one, you're the early adopter who needs it for a specific job, not a general solution. That's fine. The camera glasses make sense for creators and field documentation. The display-focused ones work if you actually need ambient information without looking down. But if you're waiting for the smart glasses that just replaces everything, that thing's still a few hardware cycles away.
