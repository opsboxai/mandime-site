The Texas Railroad Commission never pumped a drop of oil, yet it basically invented the playbook that OPEC would later copy to reshape global energy markets. Between the 1930s and 1970s, the commission controlled production across Texas by issuing "allowables" that told oil companies how much they could pump each month. The system worked like a cartel that looked nothing like a cartel: it stabilized prices, prevented the boom-bust cycles that had wrecked the industry for decades, and gave producers long-term visibility instead of the race-to-the-bottom madness of unregulated drilling.

When Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, and Venezuela formed OPEC in 1960, they weren't inventing something new. They were borrowing the Texas model and scaling it up globally. The key difference was ownership. Texas had private oil companies competing under a regulatory ceiling. OPEC countries nationalized their industries first, then applied production discipline without answering to shareholders screaming for quarterly returns. They could think in decades instead of quarters. A state-run oil ministry can swallow a low-price year if it means securing market stability and higher long-term revenue. A corporation answering to investors cannot.

That structural advantage is why OPEC outlasted every prediction of its collapse. The embargo of 1973 shocked the world, but what made it actually work was that member states controlled their own spigots. When the Texas Railroad Commission lost regulatory power in the 1970s as U.S. production peaked and Mexican and Middle Eastern oil flooded markets, the American system that had been the template collapsed. OPEC didn't, because the countries that ran it owned the wells themselves. That's the real lesson buried in the history: the Texas Railroad Commission didn't just pioneer production control. It proved that whoever owns the resource, not just whoever regulates it, holds the real power.
