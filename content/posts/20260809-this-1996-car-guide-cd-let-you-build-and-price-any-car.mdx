Before the internet killed the car salesman's information advantage, there was a CD that put the entire new car market in your hands. In 1996, a software outfit called Parsons Technology released "Car Guide," a standalone configurator that let you spec out nearly any vehicle on the market, see real prices, compare trims, and print out a buying sheet before you ever stepped foot on a lot. No browser. No ads. No waiting for pages to load on dial-up. Just you, your computer, and the raw data that dealers had been hoarding.

The wild part is how complete it actually was. You could build a car exactly how you wanted it, watch the price climb in real time as you added options, cross-compare models side by side, and export everything to paper. Features that feel basic now—filtering by price, seeing what equipment came standard on each trim, understanding the real cost delta between a base model and a loaded one—were genuinely novel in a world where your only other option was driving to three dealerships and hoping someone would be honest with you. For a guy shopping for his first new car in the mid-90s, this was the nuclear option.

What's actually remarkable is that this existed at all. A comprehensive, current vehicle database on a physical disc, updated regularly, sold for real money. It worked because the internet wasn't yet the default hub for everything. Dealers still controlled the narrative. Buyers still made major decisions based on magazine reviews and whatever a salesman told them. Then this thing showed up and flipped the script without anyone really noticing. By the time the web made this model obsolete, the power dynamic had already shifted. The customer knew what they wanted, what it cost, and what it was worth. Thirty years later, every manufacturer's site does this for free. But Car Guide did it first, offline, and arguably better because there was nothing else on the screen fighting for your attention.
