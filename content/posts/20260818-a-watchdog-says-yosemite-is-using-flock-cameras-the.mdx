A privacy watchdog caught what it says is license plate reader technology at Yosemite's entrance, the kind that captures and stores your vehicle's ID every time you roll through. The National Park Service pushes back, calling them congestion monitors. That's the tension right there: same hardware, completely different story depending on who's talking about it.

DeFlock, the group that found the cameras, says they're Flock Safety devices. Flock makes automated license plate readers that law enforcement uses nationwide, and they're notorious for the data they collect and how long they keep it. The NPS counters that these particular cameras are just tools to measure parking availability and traffic flow, part of the park's effort to manage the crush of visitors without getting people stuck for hours. The hardware might be the same, but the stated purpose matters a lot legally and ethically.

Here's what makes this worth paying attention to: even if the NPS is being straight about current use, the infrastructure is there. Flock cameras can do both jobs. Once you've got the hardware installed at park entrances, swapping the stated purpose takes nothing but a policy change. You're not installing new equipment, you're just flipping a switch on what the existing system does with the data it's already collecting. That's the real concern activists have, and it's hard to dismiss as paranoia when license plate readers exist specifically because agencies wanted exactly that kind of flexibility.

The NPS hasn't released technical details or specifics about data retention, which is probably the question that matters most. If these cameras are keeping months or years of plate data, they function as a tracking system whether that's the stated intent or not. If they're truly just counting vehicles in real time and discarding the feed, that's a different story. But the vagueness itself is telling. Public agencies that are comfortable with their surveillance practices tend to say so directly.
