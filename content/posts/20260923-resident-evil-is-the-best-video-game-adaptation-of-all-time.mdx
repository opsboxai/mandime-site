Zach Cregger just proved that video game adaptations don't have to be embarrassing. The Resident Evil film he directed made 100 million dollars worldwide, which alone is wild for a property that most people assumed was cursed after two decades of botched live-action attempts. But the real win is simpler: it's actually competent filmmaking. The action sequences are clear, the horror has weight, the performances aren't apologetic, and the script respects the source material without being a shot-for-shot museum piece.

That matters because the bar was historically on the floor. Every major game adaptation before this one read like a studio executive's notes interpreted by someone who played the original for 20 minutes. The Sonic movie fixed itself in reshoots. The Super Mario Bros. film from 1993 became a punchline that outlasted the game itself. Uncharted arrived late and DOA. Resident Evil had failed twice already with the Paul W.S. Anderson films, which made an industry assumption seem inevitable: games and movies speak different languages, and trying to translate between them is a money-burning exercise.

Cregger's version cracks something that matters. He understood that the game's DNA isn't about plot coherence or character arcs in the traditional sense. It's about atmosphere, tension, and the feeling of being trapped in a space that's trying to kill you. He built a movie around those mechanical facts instead of trying to sand them down into prestige drama. The result is a blueprint. Not every adaptation will work, and not every game is adaptable. But Resident Evil just showed that when someone actually talented takes the assignment seriously, the source material becomes an asset, not a liability. That's a lesson the industry needed.
