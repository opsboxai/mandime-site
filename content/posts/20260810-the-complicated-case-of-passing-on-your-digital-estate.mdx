Most guys never think about what happens to their Netflix password, their photos, their crypto wallet, or their email when they die. And that's the problem. Your digital life is worth real money and holds actual memories, but there's basically no legal framework that treats it like an inheritance.

Here's the mess: platforms like Google, Apple, and Facebook have their own rules for what happens to your accounts. Some let you name a legacy contact. Others just lock the door forever. Your heirs can't automatically access anything, even with a will. A photo library scattered across cloud services, a Spotify account, maybe Bitcoin sitting in an exchange, Discord servers you moderate, Reddit accounts with years of history—none of it has a clear legal owner after you're gone. Your family might never find it, or find it and have no way in.

The practical fix starts now. Make a list of what actually matters: email, photos, financial accounts, cryptocurrency, social media, subscription services. Write down usernames and where the passwords live, ideally in a secure password manager your executor can access. Then check each platform's settings. Google lets you add a legacy contact who can access certain data. Facebook has a similar option. Some services let you delete your account entirely after death if you prefer. Others offer nothing. For the ones that don't, at least your family will know what exists and can try working with the company directly, which has better odds than finding a hidden account by accident three years later.

This isn't glamorous estate planning. It won't make your will any longer. But it's the difference between your family inheriting your life's photos and memories, and losing access to them forever because they don't know the password to an email account tied to everything else.
