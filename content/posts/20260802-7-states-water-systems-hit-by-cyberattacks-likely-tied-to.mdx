Seven states just found out their water systems got hacked, and the FBI is pretty confident Iran's behind it. We're talking operational technology that actually controls the pipes and treatment facilities—not just some database sitting on a server somewhere. That's the kind of access that matters, the kind that could theoretically let someone mess with chlorine levels or shut down distribution if they wanted to push things that far.

Nobody's reported actual damage or contamination yet, which is the only good news here. But the fact that a foreign adversary got deep enough into water infrastructure to trigger a federal alert says something blunt about how exposed critical systems still are. These aren't tiny rural water boards either. This is infrastructure that affects actual people's ability to turn on a tap and drink. Iran's been probing US critical infrastructure for years—power grids, oil and gas, hospitals—so water systems were probably just another box to check on the list. The difference now is they got caught doing it, which at least means the agencies involved are paying attention.

The broader play here matters more than any single intrusion. If Iran's willing to test the defenses around water treatment in seven different states, they're mapping what works and what doesn't. It's reconnaissance dressed up as an attack. Defenders will patch, alerts will go out, and life moves on. But the next time someone tries it, they'll know more about how to get in and how long they can stay invisible. That's the real threat—not tomorrow's attack, but what they learned today about how to launch the next one.
