The National Park Service is sitting on a 15-billion-dollar maintenance backlog, and it's not getting better. Roads are cracking, campground facilities are degrading, visitor centers are aging out, and trail systems that draw millions of people annually are literally falling apart. This isn't a minor budget shortfall or deferred maintenance that can wait until next fiscal year. Mary Andino's reporting makes clear this is infrastructure collapse happening in real time at some of the most visited parks in the country.

The scale matters here. When a major park road needs repaving but the NPS can't fund it, that's not just inconvenience. It's safety risk, reduced access for people who plan their vacations around these places, and accelerated deterioration of what amounts to America's shared outdoor property. Popular parks like Yellowstone, the Grand Canyon, and Great Smoky Mountains are dealing with aging water systems, deteriorating buildings, and trails that need work that isn't happening. A visitor paying for a permit or campground reservation is essentially funding a park that's operating on fumes.

The real problem is structural. The NPS annual budget doesn't scale with the size of the park system or the volume of visitors. Every year they're choosing between immediate maintenance and long-term projects. Deferred maintenance costs compound, meaning that 15-billion-dollar gap grows faster than it shrinks unless someone actually funds it. Until Congress treats this as what it is, fundamental infrastructure failure, parks will continue aging into worse condition while visitors show up expecting the experience they've been promised.
