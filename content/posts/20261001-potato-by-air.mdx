Wing's Hummingbird 7000W-B carries less than 2.7 pounds at a time, which means your grocery delivery isn't coming by drone anytime soon, but your french fries might. The FAA just cleared this thing for beyond-visual-line-of-sight operations, which is the regulatory green light that turns a novelty into actual infrastructure. It weighs under 15 pounds, runs 16 propellers split between horizontal and vertical thrust, and hits 60 mph across a six-mile radius. That's not a toy. That's a drone built to solve a real logistics problem at real scale.

The catch is the payload. 2.7 pounds means you're not replacing your Uber Eats order, but you are looking at the exact use case that will dominate the next five years of delivery: small, high-margin items that people want fast. Restaurant sides. Pharmacy pickups. Convenience goods. The economics work because the drone doesn't need a driver, can make multiple runs per hour, and operates in airspace that was completely closed to commerce two years ago. Wing has been testing this in Australia and the Middle East while American regulators moved at their usual pace. Now they're not.

What matters here is the precedent. The FAA approval signals that autonomous beyond-visual-line-of-sight drone delivery isn't coming eventually. It's here. Every major logistics company just got notice that the last-mile game is about to change, and the companies that figured out how to move small packages at scale with minimal infrastructure just became a threat to traditional delivery models. That's why this matters more than the specs.
