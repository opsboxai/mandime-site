GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide and tirzepatide are being prescribed for something that has nothing to do with their original purpose. They weren't designed for weight loss. They were developed to help diabetics manage blood sugar. But somewhere in the last few years, the conversation shifted. Now they're being used as a preventive health tool by people without diabetes, people who just want to not get fat or have a heart attack.

The comparison to statins is real, and it's worth sitting with for a second. Statins became the default drug doctors handed out to millions of men starting in the 1990s, whether or not they'd actually had heart problems yet. The logic was simple: high cholesterol is a risk factor for heart disease, so lower it preemptively. It mostly worked. Statins became routine. Now GLP-1s are following the same trajectory, except the mechanism is different. They suppress appetite, lower blood sugar, reduce inflammation, and seem to have direct protective effects on the heart and cardiovascular system. Early data suggests they might prevent heart attack and stroke in people with obesity or metabolic risk factors, even if they never become diabetic.

Here's what matters: we're watching a class of drug transition from treatment to prevention in real time. And we don't fully know the long-term effects of taking them for years or decades. We know they work for weight loss and blood sugar control in the short term. We're starting to see cardiovascular benefits. But the data on 10 or 20 years of continuous use in otherwise healthy men doesn't exist yet. That's not a reason to avoid them, but it's a reason to be honest about what we don't know. Statins had the same problem in 1995. The difference is that statins were cheap and boring. GLP-1s are expensive, in high demand, and have become a status symbol. That changes the conversation faster than the science does.
