Steven Strogatz has spent decades teaching people that math is beautiful. He's written bestselling books, hosted podcasts, and built a career on the premise that complex ideas can be made elegant and accessible. Then AI started solving problems faster than he could explain them.

The mathematician's alarm isn't abstract. When tools like AlphaProof and other AI systems started cracking theorems that would take human mathematicians months or years to verify, it wasn't a philosophical concern. It was a professional earthquake. Strogatz coauthored a book exploring how mathematics is moving beyond human understanding and verification. He wrote it thinking the timeline was distant. Then the timeline arrived. He's now grappling with a reality where the work mathematicians do might become impossible to follow, let alone teach, in any meaningful way. If an AI proves something true but no human can check its work, what does that actually mean for math as a discipline?

The deeper tension Strogatz faces reveals something most people miss about AI breakthroughs. This isn't about robots taking jobs or algorithms getting smarter at pattern matching. This is about the foundational languages humans use to understand reality itself. Math was supposed to be the one thing machines couldn't touch without humans understanding every step. That assumption is dead. And for someone who's built his entire career on making those steps visible and beautiful, that's genuinely terrifying. Not because AI is dangerous in the dramatic sense, but because it's fundamentally altered what math is for and who gets to do it.
