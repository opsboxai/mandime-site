Researchers just used AI to design 16 viruses from scratch, and they work. These aren't theoretical constructs pulled from a database—they're functional bacteriophages, the kind that kill bacteria. The team fed an AI system information about existing phages, let it learn the patterns, and it generated novel sequences that, when synthesized and tested in the lab, actually did the job they were designed for.

The immediate application is obvious and urgent: antibiotic resistance is killing people. Bacteria are evolving faster than we can make new drugs to kill them. Bacteriophages attack bacteria through a completely different mechanism, which means resistant strains can't just shrug them off the way they do with antibiotics. Using AI to rapidly design custom phages tailored to specific resistant bacteria could be the kind of precision medicine we desperately need. It's not theoretical anymore. It works.

But here's the real problem: the technology to do this is democratizing faster than anyone can regulate it. The researchers published their method. That's good for science. It's also good for someone in a basement with a laptop and access to a DNA synthesis service. We've gone from "make a virus" being something only a well-funded lab with biosafety protocols could attempt to something that's becoming algorithmically routine. The guardrails—ethical review, government oversight, international agreements—are still stuck in the old paradigm where creating a pathogen required resources most actors didn't have. Now it's becoming a software problem. The regulatory lag is real and it's accelerating.
