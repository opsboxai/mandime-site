A Piper PA-31 twin-engine plane went down near Farmington, New Mexico in 2023, killing all seven people aboard. The crash investigators found something unusual in the wreckage: the aircraft's navigation systems had been jammed. Not by enemy combatants. By the U.S. military's own electronic warfare equipment being tested nearby.

This is the collision point nobody really talks about when we celebrate drone warfare and advanced military tech. The Air Force and Navy have been ramping up GPS jamming exercises across American airspace for years, ostensibly to train for peer conflicts and test defenses against adversarial spoofing. The problem is civilian pilots, especially those flying older aircraft without the latest backup systems, are flying blind when that happens. A commercial airliner or a small charter plane loses its primary navigation tool, and suddenly the pilot is working with backup instruments that may not be accurate enough for low-visibility approaches.

The FAA knew jamming was happening. The military issued notices. But the gap between a bureaucratic memo and the actual guy in the cockpit is wide. Pilots might not see a notice. They might not understand how jamming works or what it means for their specific aircraft. They definitely don't expect it at 1,000 feet over rural New Mexico.

What's wild is how long this has gone on without a major reckoning. Every time the military needs to test electronic warfare capabilities, they notify airspace users in theory. In practice, civilian aviation and military operations occupy the same actual sky, and when those worlds collide, it's the people in the smaller aircraft who pay the price. The FAA and the military have been talking about fixing this for years. Meanwhile, people are still dying because the system for keeping critical information flowing to the people who need it most is broken.
