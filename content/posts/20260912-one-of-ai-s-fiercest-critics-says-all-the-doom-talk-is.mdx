Timnit Gebru, one of the sharpest voices pushing back on AI hype, just said something most people won't want to hear: all the apocalypse talk coming from AI companies might be strategic distraction. Not genuine concern about killer robots. Distraction.

Her argument is cleaner than it sounds. When OpenAI, Google, or Anthropic spend oxygen talking about existential risk and superintelligence, they're operating in a space where they control the narrative. It's theoretical, it's far away, it's something for governments and philosophers to wrestle with. What they're not talking about as loudly are the problems happening right now. Autonomous weapons that actually work. Training data scraped without permission. Systems that amplify discrimination. The stuff you can point to and say, "This broke something today." That's harder to defend.

The tactic is almost elegant: promise massive future peril while staying vague about present-day damage. It repositions criticism as fearmongering rather than accountability. A researcher pointing out that an AI model is biased against women in hiring? That's a specific, fixable complaint. A researcher warning about superintelligent AGI? That's theoretical, unquantifiable, easier to dismiss as speculation. One is a problem for product teams. The other is a problem for humanity in 2087.

Gebru's not arguing that AI can't be dangerous. She's saying the conversation has been engineered to protect the industry by moving the goal posts to a place where no one can actually verify whether the claimed dangers are real. And the thing is, it's hard to argue she's wrong about the timing and the messaging. Whether you think AI existential risk matters or not, the fact that it became the dominant safety conversation right as the companies got big enough to shape public discourse is, at minimum, worth noticing.
