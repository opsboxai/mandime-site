Meta's throwing a nine-figure bet at an AI assistant called Muse, and the timing says everything about where they stand right now. While OpenAI's been cleaning up with ChatGPT and Google's been quietly dominating the infrastructure play, Meta's been stuck playing catch-up in a race it technically helped start. Muse is their answer: a personal assistant designed to be accessible enough that regular people actually use it, not just tech nerds tinkering in Discord.

The play here is obvious but not wrong. Meta owns the distribution. Billions of people already live inside Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp every single day. If they can fold a useful AI assistant into those platforms instead of asking users to download yet another app or sign up for another service, they've got a massive advantage everyone else has to build from scratch. It's the same reason their pivot to AI matters at all. Raw model quality matters less than reach, and Meta still has reach nobody else can touch.

But here's the catch: accessibility is table stakes now, not a differentiator. What matters is whether Muse actually does anything people can't get elsewhere. ChatGPT works. Claude works. Google's Gemini works. The question isn't whether Meta can make an AI assistant. It's whether they can make one good enough that people choose it over what they're already using, or whether this is just another feature to keep people scrolling longer. For a company that's spent the last year talking about AI but hasn't shipped a clear win yet, Muse is a necessary move. Whether it's enough is a different story.
