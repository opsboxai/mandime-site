Flock Safety built its reputation on a simple pitch: American-made cameras that could help cops catch criminals faster. That messaging worked. The company went from startup to unicorn status with cameras deployed across thousands of jurisdictions nationwide. But reporting by Paresh Dave at Wired found something less clear than the marketing suggested. Flock is remarkably opaque about where its license plate readers are actually assembled, and the company seems to have quietly walked back earlier claims about domestic manufacturing.

This matters because it's not just about buying local. When the government relies on a surveillance tool to identify people's movements, knowing who built it and where becomes a legitimate security question. Foreign assembly, foreign components, foreign code updates—these aren't abstract policy concerns. They're vectors for tampering, backdoors, data interception. If you're going to let a private company track every car moving through your city, you probably want to know whether that hardware was assembled in a factory with oversight or in a place where another government could insert something nasty into the supply chain.

The gap between Flock's original messaging and its current opacity reveals how startup hype often works in practice. They tell a story that resonates, build on it, get capital flowing, hit scale—and then suddenly the details that seemed important at launch become inconvenient. For government agencies writing contracts, it's a reminder to ask harder questions about what "made in the USA" actually means before the cameras are already mounted on the pole.
