Andy Yen built his reputation on a simple bet: that privacy should be the default, not a luxury. Proton started in 2014 as encrypted email for the paranoid. Now it's everywhere—encrypted storage, VPN, password manager—all running on the premise that your data shouldn't be readable to anyone but you. So when Yen started talking about integrating AI into Proton's products, people asked the obvious question: how do you run machine learning on data you can't actually read?

The answer is neither simple nor reassuring. You can't. Not really. Training AI models requires access to raw data, which means decryption, which means someone has to see what's inside. Yen's move is a direct collision between two things people want but can't easily have together: the ability to use AI assistants for actual tasks, and the guarantee that no one—not Proton, not the government, not anyone—can see your files. His solution involves federated learning and on-device processing, which sounds good in a whitepaper. In practice, it means Proton is betting it can build AI tools useful enough to matter while keeping decryption to an absolute minimum. It's an engineering problem that might not have a real answer, and Yen's essentially admitting that privacy and AI coexistence will require choosing which parts of privacy you're willing to sacrifice. That's the harder conversation than whether it's possible at all.
