Flock's CEO didn't show up to a Senate hearing about the company's AI surveillance network, which is exactly the kind of move that makes lawmakers lose their minds. According to the hearing, Flock has become the dominant player in real-time license plate recognition technology, with cameras spread across the country feeding data into a system that can track vehicle movements at scale. The absence was telling. Senate Judiciary's crime and counterterrorism subcommittee made it clear: there's one name senators keep coming back to when they talk about surveillance overreach, and it's Flock.

The company's infrastructure is already woven into American policing. Police departments use Flock to flag vehicles in real time, cross-reference stolen car databases, and theoretically help solve crimes faster. But the actual guardrails around how that data gets used, who has access to it, and what happens to it long-term are all still being figured out. Senators want answers about whether this is a tool that helps cops catch bad guys or a mass surveillance apparatus dressed up as public safety tech. The fact that Flock's leadership ghosted the hearing suggests they might not have great answers.

This isn't just Washington theater. License plate readers are already everywhere, and most people driving have no idea. The question now is whether Flock's dominance in the space forces the government to actually set rules around how this data flows, who can query it, and for how long it gets stored. A no-show from the CEO only makes that regulation more likely.
