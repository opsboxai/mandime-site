A fire lookout tower that's been staffed every summer for over a century burned to the ground on July 31st. Flag Point, perched high in Oregon's Mount Hood National Forest, was one of those places that felt permanent in the way old things do, until it wasn't. It's gone now, along with the continuity it represented. And it's not alone.

The wildfires tearing through the Pacific Northwest aren't just taking out trees. They're erasing infrastructure that defines how people experience these mountains. Timberline Lodge, the iconic ski resort on Hood's south side, sits in the direct path of escalating fire activity. If that goes, you're not just losing a place to ski; you're losing a destination that's been anchoring the region since the 1930s, a spot where families have built their traditions across generations. Smaller ranger stations, historic cabins, and trail systems are already threatened. The cumulative loss isn't just about nostalgia. It's about access, jobs, and the physical anchors that turn a forest into a place people actually use and care about.

What makes this different from previous fire seasons is the scale and speed. The wildfires are bigger, they're arriving earlier, and they're hitting elevation zones that rarely burned before. Climate patterns are shifting what "fire season" even means anymore. Mount Hood isn't some remote peak anymore; it's a working landscape with lodges, ski operations, tens of thousands of visitors a year, and institutional memory baked into its structures. Losing Flag Point was losing a working piece of history. Losing Timberline would be losing one of the anchors of the entire region's outdoor economy.
