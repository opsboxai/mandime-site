Meta's ad network ran paid promotions for an app that used AI to generate fake nude images of women, including sitting US politicians. The app, which featured deepfake pornography of at least one recognizable female politician, was actively advertised on Meta's platforms until WIRED's inquiry prompted action. Apple then yanked it from the App Store, but the speed of removal only highlights how long it stayed live in the first place.

This is the part where most coverage pauses to discuss "the dangers of deepfake technology" in broad strokes. Skip that. The actual story is simpler and uglier: Meta's ad system accepted payment to promote sexual abuse material targeting specific, named women in positions of power. The company didn't catch it. A journalist had to ask them about it before anything happened. That's not a content moderation failure in the abstract sense. That's a platform knowingly monetizing the nonconsensual sexualization of women and then moving on only when publicly confronted.

The app itself is small potatoes compared to the ecosystem that funded it. Dozens of these tools exist. They're cheap to build, easy to distribute, and profitable because there's demand from people who want to humiliate specific women. Meta's ad system is supposed to catch this stuff. Instead, it became part of the supply chain. Until someone forced the issue, nobody stopped it.
