A senior AI safety researcher just walked out of one of the world's most funded AI labs, and his reason wasn't a better offer or burnout. Jacob Coxon spent years at Anthropic working on alignment—basically, the problem of making sure superintelligent AI systems do what humans actually want them to do—and he's now convinced the industry has maybe a handful of years to solve it before the stakes get too high to manage.

The brutal part: he thinks most people inside the labs building this stuff don't actually disagree with him. Coxon describes Anthropic's safety team as a "mini Manhattan project," which sounds like a compliment until you realize what the Manhattan Project was actually for. These are smart people racing toward something transformative, and they know the clock is ticking. The difference between Coxon and the people still in the building is that he's decided the labs themselves—despite their explicit focus on safety—can't actually solve this problem fast enough, or maybe at all.

The core issue is almost jarringly simple: the systems are getting smarter, the timelines are collapsing, and alignment research is still operating on old assumptions about how long everyone has. Coxon isn't saying AI labs are reckless. He's saying they're rational actors trapped in a structure that doesn't reward the kind of slow, paranoid caution the problem actually requires. When your company's survival depends on moving fast and staying competitive, the pressure to ship systems before you've fully understood them is just physics, not malice.

What makes this different from the usual doomsday AI discourse is that it's coming from inside the building. Coxon wasn't fired for being alarmist. He quit because he thinks the people he respects most in the field have correctly identified the problem and then chosen to operate as if they haven't.
