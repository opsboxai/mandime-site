A satellite that's been hunting gamma-ray bursts for twenty years is about to burn up in Earth's atmosphere, and NASA just gave it one last assignment: keep observing until the moment it dies.

The Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has been falling out of orbit for months. Its fuel is gone. The thrusters that kept it pointed at distant explosions across the cosmos are useless now. So instead of letting it drift silent into oblivion, the team fired up its telescopes again and pointed them at the sky one final time. These aren't sentimental last looks. They're working observations, real data being collected on real targets, right up until atmospheric drag rips the satellite apart.

This is what the end of a space mission actually looks like when you don't have a choice. Swift launched in 2004 to catch gamma-ray bursts, the most violent explosions in the universe. It's been doing that job for two decades, and it's been good at it. But satellites don't get retirement parties. They fall. When they do, mission control doesn't just watch them go dark. They squeeze every last second of utility out of them first. The final observations might not be the most critical data Swift ever collected, but they're data nonetheless, wrung from a machine that's already on its way down. It's the opposite of waste, a last-second pivot from decommissioning to one more job before the end.

Reentry should happen sometime in 2025. When it does, most of Swift will burn up. Some debris might survive the fall. But until that moment comes, the telescopes keep working, the sensors keep recording, and a satellite that's already living on borrowed time does what it was built to do.
