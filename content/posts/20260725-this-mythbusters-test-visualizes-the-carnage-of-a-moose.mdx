The popular theory goes like this: if you're going to hit a moose, you might as well lean into it. Accelerate, lower your center of gravity, and let the animal go over the hood instead of through your windshield. It sounds like the kind of backwards logic that actually works, the kind of thing that makes you feel clever for knowing it. MythBusters tested it anyway, and the results are instructive precisely because they don't match the internet's conventional wisdom.

They built a 620-pound rubber moose dummy and ran it through a series of collision scenarios at different speeds. The physics are unforgiving. A moose's legs are relatively thin and brittle compared to the mass of its body. When you hit one at highway speed, those legs snap immediately. The massive torso doesn't stop, doesn't bounce away cleanly. It comes through whatever barrier sits between it and the people inside the car. Whether you're doing 55 mph or 75 mph, a moose collision is a catastrophic event for the vehicle and everyone in it. The only real variable is how many times the animal's body tumbles across your roof and through your windows on the way through.

The accelerate theory fails because it's built on a misunderstanding of how impact angles work at scale. You're not outsmarting physics by adding speed. You're just ensuring that when a thousand-pound ungulate makes contact with your car, it does so with more relative velocity. Braking doesn't guarantee safety either, but it reduces the energy of the collision and gives you the smallest possible chance of surviving intact. The actual answer is the boring one: see the moose early, brake hard, and hope the animal moves. Everything else is just rationalization after the fact.
