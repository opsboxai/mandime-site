An avalanche on Broad Peak in January killed ten climbers in one of the deadliest incidents on a high-altitude peak in years. Five of them were Sherpas. That matters not just because it's tragic, but because it exposes something most climbers don't think about when they're booking that expedition: the economic collapse that hits the families left behind.

The Sherpas who die on these mountains aren't wealthy professionals taking calculated risks as a career choice. They're breadwinners in Nepal making a few thousand dollars per expedition, often working multiple seasons a year because they have to. When one doesn't come home, his family loses the salary that fed them. No insurance. No death benefit. No safety net. Just gone. And the climbing industry, which profits heavily from their labor, has historically done almost nothing about it.

The crowdfunding campaigns gathering momentum now are grassroots fixes to a structural problem. They're necessary, urgent, and also kind of embarrassing. When a professional climbing guide dies in the Alps, there are protocols, compensation, institutional support. When a Sherpa dies, the response depends on the goodwill of climbers who knew them or felt guilty enough to donate. That's not a system. That's charity pretending to be justice. The real conversation should be about why expeditions aren't legally required to provide death benefits, why gear companies profiting from Himalayan content aren't funding families, and why the climbing world accepts this gap between how it treats Western climbers and how it treats the people doing the hardest work on the mountains.
