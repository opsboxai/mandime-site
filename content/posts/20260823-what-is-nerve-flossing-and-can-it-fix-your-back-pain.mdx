Nerve flossing sounds like something a dentist invented after too many espressos, but it's actually a legit physical therapy technique that's been around for decades. The idea is simple: your nerves can get stuck or irritated where they pass through tight spaces in your body, and gentle movement can free them up the same way flossing clears space between your teeth. The catch, according to Dean Stattmann's reporting, is that it only works for a specific slice of people dealing with back pain, not the broad fix it's sometimes marketed as.

Here's what matters. If you've got genuine nerve compression—the kind where a nerve is actually pinched or restricted in its range of motion—nerve flossing exercises can help by encouraging the nerve to glide smoothly through surrounding tissue again. But if your back pain is structural, muscular, or just plain mechanical tension, you're wasting time doing these moves. The technique takes maybe a few minutes and requires zero equipment, which is why it's become popular on fitness social media, but that accessibility has also led to people treating it like a universal back pain cure. It's not.

The real move is figuring out whether your pain is actually nerve-related before you commit. That usually requires a physical therapist or doctor who can actually assess what's happening. If it is nerve-based tightness, nerve flossing can be genuinely useful as part of a broader plan. If it isn't, you're just doing stretches that might feel good in the moment but won't solve the underlying problem. Worth knowing the difference before you start.
