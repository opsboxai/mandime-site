Rivian's Also TM-B isn't trying to be a car on two wheels, which is exactly why it works. Michael Frank took one out and came back genuinely impressed by how well the company nailed the fundamentals: the motor feels natural, the battery integration doesn't feel bolted-on like it does on most e-bikes, and the geometry actually lets you ride without feeling like you're piloting some oversized shopping cart. That's rarer than it sounds. Most e-bike startups treat the electric part as the entire story and forget that the bike still has to feel like a bike.

What's wild is how little fanfare this got compared to Rivian's automotive work. The e-bike space has spent the last decade obsessed with speed and range specs while largely ignoring how the thing actually rides day to day. Also seems to have swung the other way: these bikes prioritize the experience of riding them, which means the motor doesn't fight you, the handling stays responsive even loaded, and the battery weight sits low where it belongs. That's the kind of boring-sounding design decision that separates a tool you actually enjoy from gear you tolerate.

The bigger picture matters here. The automotive industry has attracted massive engineering talent because the stakes are high and the budgets are real. E-bikes have mostly relied on whoever could slap together a frame and a Bosch motor fast enough to sell it. Rivian bringing actual automotive design thinking to something as simple as a two-wheeled commuter is worth paying attention to. It suggests the category is finally ready for the kind of iteration and obsession that made electric cars move from novelty to inevitable.
