The Trump administration is caught between two competing instincts on Chinese AI, and neither one looks especially coherent yet. On one side, there's the instinct to clamp down hard—restrict access, ban the models, treat it like a national security threat the way they're treating chips and semiconductors. On the other, there's the pull toward letting the market breathe and not overregulating an industry that's still moving fast enough that yesterday's restrictions become today's irrelevance.

What makes this actually matter is that Chinese AI is getting good. Not theoretical-future-good. Now-good. Models like those coming out of companies in Beijing are closing gaps that seemed insurmountable two years ago, which means the window to act on this—if action is what the administration wants—is narrower than it looks. Every month of delay is a month of Chinese iteration. But every month of hasty policy is also a month of potential American companies tangled in rules that might not even accomplish what they're meant to.

The real problem is that China's AI advantage isn't primarily about stealing models or leaking weights anymore. It's about speed, scale, and the willingness to train on datasets the U.S. would face legal nightmares over. You can't regulate your way out of a work ethic problem. Which means whatever the White House lands on—and they will eventually land on something—will probably look less like a solution and more like a gesture toward the voters who care about this stuff while the actual competition plays out somewhere else entirely.
