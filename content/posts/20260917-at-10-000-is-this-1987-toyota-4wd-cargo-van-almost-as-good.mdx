A 1987 Toyota Van with over 200,000 miles on the clock is asking ten grand, which is the kind of number that makes you stop and do the math. These things were built to outlast their owners' patience, not their engines, so the fact that this one still runs is already winning half the battle. But here's the thing: ten thousand dollars for a 37-year-old cargo van is either a steal or a setup, depending on what you actually need and what "surprisingly good condition" really means when Rob Emslie says it.

The math works if you're looking at this as a cheap 4WD platform that'll take you to places your sensible SUV won't go. First-gen Vans have a cult following for a reason. They're simple, they're easy to work on, and parts are dirt cheap because Toyota sold millions of them and nobody's let them die yet. If the rust is cosmetic, the frame is solid, and the engine fires up without drama, you're looking at maybe five grand in actual value for the rig itself. The other half of that ten grand is what the seller is charging for the privilege of not having to find it yourself on Facebook Marketplace.

The real question isn't whether it's a good deal. It's whether you've got the time and tolerance for what comes next. A 200k-mile Toyota running today doesn't mean it'll run tomorrow without work. Seals dry out, hoses crack, carburetor gums up after sitting. The asking price assumes you either already know how to keep one of these alive or you're willing to learn fast. If you do, and if that paint and frame really are solid, you're not buying a restored van. You're buying the skeleton of something you'll spend the next five years turning into what you actually want. That's worth ten grand to the right person. Maybe you.
