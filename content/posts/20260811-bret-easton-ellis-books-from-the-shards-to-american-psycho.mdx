Bret Easton Ellis has spent four decades writing books that make people uncomfortable, and that's exactly the point. From the clinical detachment of Patrick Bateman's narration in American Psycho to the fractured, diary-entry structure of his latest novel The Shards, Ellis doesn't write to comfort you. He writes to put a mirror up to the shallow, violent underbelly of American culture and make you sit with it.

The ranking game is always a bit silly when you're talking about a writer this consistent, but the real conversation worth having is this: which Ellis novel actually stayed with you, and why. American Psycho is the obvious cultural touchstone, the one everyone's heard of even if they haven't read it. But there's an argument that Some People Are Not Made to Be Mothers or The Informers get closer to what Ellis is actually interested in—the small, cruel moments of human behavior that don't need a serial killer narrator to feel genuinely disturbing. The Shards proved Ellis hasn't softened with age, either. Published when he was in his sixties, it's as sharp and claustrophobic as anything he wrote in the 1990s, just with the clarity that only decades of living in this world can bring.

If you're coming to Ellis through one of the recent film or TV adaptations, the temptation is to treat his books like source material for content. They're not. They're precise, unsettling pieces of writing that work because Ellis refuses to look away or let you off the hook. Start with American Psycho if you want to understand the cultural conversation. But read the others if you actually want to understand Ellis.
