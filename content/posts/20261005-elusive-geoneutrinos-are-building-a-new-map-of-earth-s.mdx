The Earth's interior is radioactive, and we've barely been able to prove it. For decades, scientists knew that uranium, thorium, and potassium deep underground were decaying and generating heat that drives plate tectonics, volcanic eruptions, and the magnetic field that keeps us alive. But measuring it directly was basically impossible. Now a global network of detectors buried around the planet is finally catching the ghostly particles released by that decay, called geoneutrinos, and the data is rewriting what we know about how much heat is actually down there and where it's concentrated.

The catch is that geoneutrinos are almost impossibly hard to detect. These particles rarely interact with anything and pass through matter as if it doesn't exist. It takes a tank of ultra-pure liquid scintillator the size of a small house, buried deep underground to shield it from cosmic rays, to catch maybe a handful of them per year. But catch them they do. Recent measurements from detectors in Japan, Italy, and elsewhere have produced the most substantial data yet on the radioactive composition of Earth's crust and upper mantle. The numbers confirm that radioactive decay contributes more heat to planetary dynamics than some models predicted, and they're starting to reveal geographic patterns about where that heat is concentrated.

This matters because the more precisely we know what's heating the planet from the inside, the better we understand earthquakes, volcanic hotspots, and the deep forces that shape continents. It also touches on a fundamental question in geology: how much of Earth's internal energy comes from leftover heat from the planet's formation versus active radioactive decay right now. The geoneutrino network isn't just mapping a curiosity. It's building the first real picture of the furnace beneath your feet.
