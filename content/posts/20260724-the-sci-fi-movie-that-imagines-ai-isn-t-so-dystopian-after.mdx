A director who's spent two decades making films about family and loss just made a movie where an AI brings a dead child back. Hirokazu Kore-eda's new film imagines something we don't usually see in science fiction: a technology that actually delivers on its promise without turning everything to ash in the process.

The setup is brutal. A seven-year-old dies in an accident. His parents are destroyed. Then they're offered a chance to have him back, but not in the way you'd expect. Instead of resurrection or some creepy digital copy pretending to be human, they get an AI that learns who their son was and becomes a companion to their grief rather than a replacement for him. That's the opposite of how Hollywood usually plays this hand. Usually AI either enslaves us or it dies learning to love us. Here it just... helps.

What makes this work is that Kore-eda isn't interested in whether the technology is real or possible. He's interested in whether it's kind. And that's a radical question to ask in a genre that's spent decades terrifying us about silicon valley. His previous films have always been about what we owe each other when loss happens, when families break, when the people we love are gone. An AI movie from him doesn't become a Terminator scenario. It becomes a meditation on whether there's any version of connection that's better than nothing at all.

The film takes the one thing AI movies usually treat as the beginning of the end and uses it as the beginning of the beginning. Not recovery. Not healing. Just a different way to sit with impossible pain. That's not dystopia. That's almost tender.
