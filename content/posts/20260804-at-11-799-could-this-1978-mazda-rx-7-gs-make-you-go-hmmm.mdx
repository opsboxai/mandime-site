The asking price on this 1978 RX-7 GS is $11,799, and that number matters because it puts a legitimate slice of rotary history within reach of someone who actually wants to drive it instead of trailer-queen it. These cars have spent the last decade climbing in value, so eleven grand for a first-generation RX-7 in decent shape isn't the steal it would have been in 2015, but it's also not the gatekeeping price that keeps them locked in air-conditioned garages.

The real story here is that the Wankel rotary engine is still the weirdest, most polarizing powertrain ever bolted into a production car, and that 1.1-liter twin-rotor sitting in this GS is the exact reason people either become obsessed with Mazdas or swear them off forever. Compared to the piston-engine orthodoxy of the era, the rotary felt alien. It screamed to 6,600 RPM like it had something to prove. It needed oil in the fuel. It had spark plug issues. And somehow, despite all of that, it made the RX-7 feel nimble and purposeful in a way that American V8s and Japanese inline-fours didn't quite nail.

A 1978 GS isn't going to set your hair on fire performance-wise, but that's almost beside the point. What you're actually buying is the last practical moment to own a car that represents a specific era of automotive experimentation, before Mazda themselves abandoned the rotary for general production and before the collector market fully calcified around these things. At this price, you're not overpaying for rarity. You're just paying to find out if the rotary legend actually deserves the mythology or if you'll spend the next two years chasing vacuum leaks and wondering why you didn't buy an S-series instead.
