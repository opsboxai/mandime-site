Someone is making real money betting on whether your house burns down. That's the actual situation Congress just woke up to, and they're not wrong to be alarmed.

Prediction markets have exploded in the last few years, turning everything from election outcomes to sports injuries into tradable contracts. The logic is clean: let people bet real money on uncertain events and the market price becomes a crowdsourced forecast. Works great for most things. But when the event in question is an active wildfire spreading toward populated areas, the incentive structure breaks down in exactly the way it sounds. If you've got money riding on a fire spreading further or burning hotter, you have skin in the game to hope it does. That's not a market correcting information inefficiency. That's a perverse incentive built into the foundation of the bet itself.

A handful of senators flagged this to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the federal agency that oversees prediction markets. They want the CFTC to block new wildfire contracts outright. The argument isn't theoretical. Wildland firefighters already operate under brutal conditions with limited resources. Communities in fire zones can't afford distractions or complications to their evacuation and response efforts. If there's even a small chance that someone's financial interest is clouding information flow, decision-making, or community trust during an active emergency, that's not worth whatever liquidity the market provides.

The prediction market industry will argue this is overreach and that bets don't actually cause fires. They're right on the second point. But they're sidestepping the real issue: some markets shouldn't exist just because they're technically possible to create. The question Congress is asking is whether the financial incentive to hope a disaster gets worse is something a stable society should be comfortable with. The answer is pretty obvious.
