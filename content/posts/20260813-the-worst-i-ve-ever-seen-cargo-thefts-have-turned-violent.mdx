Cargo thieves in California have started hunting trucks carrying AI hardware with a level of violence and coordination that law enforcement hasn't seen before. In one incident, a group intercepted a shipment of servers worth nearly half a million dollars by ramming the delivery truck off the road. In another, thieves followed a rig for miles before making their move. These aren't opportunistic highway robberies anymore. They're organized, targeted operations where criminals know exactly what they're after and how much it's worth.

The targets tell the real story here. AI chips and data center equipment have become so valuable that they've crossed a threshold where the risk-reward math changes. A single container of GPUs or custom processors can be worth more than a house. When that much value sits in a truck for twelve hours, moving across state lines with skeleton crews, the incentive structure gets dark fast. Thieves who might have once hit warehouses for electronics or cargo are now running what amounts to military-style intercepts.

What's strange is how visible this has become. Law enforcement and industry insiders are going on record about it, which usually only happens when a problem has already gotten serious enough that hiding it doesn't matter anymore. The thieves are getting better at logistics, better at timing, and better at knowing where to find these shipments in the first place. That suggests either someone inside the supply chain is feeding information, or the thieves have gotten good enough at tracking that they don't need help. Either way, it's a new problem for an industry that spent years optimizing for speed and cost but not security.
