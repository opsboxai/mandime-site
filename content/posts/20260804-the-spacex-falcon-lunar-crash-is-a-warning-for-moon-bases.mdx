A SpaceX rocket stage slammed into the moon in March 2022, and nobody really noticed. The impact happened on the far side, silent and invisible from Earth, but it left a crater and a problem that's about to get a lot worse: the moon is starting to accumulate debris, and there's no traffic control up there.

This wasn't an accident or a failure. SpaceX's Falcon 9 upper stage was already spent when it hit, left in lunar orbit after a launch years earlier. But it highlights something that's about to matter enormously. As more countries and private companies race to land on the moon, they're leaving behind fuel tanks, landing gear, broken equipment, and stages in lunar orbit. When those things eventually fall, they hit with no atmosphere to slow them down, creating craters and throwing up dust and rock fragments that could damage future habitats, solar panels, or life support systems.

The math is brutal. Right now there are maybe a dozen pieces of debris in lunar orbit, mostly old Apollo hardware and a few stray rocket stages. But the timeline is about to accelerate hard. NASA wants crewed lunar bases. China is building one. Private landers are multiplying. Within the next decade, that dozen could become a hundred. And unlike Earth orbit, where debris can eventually burn up in the atmosphere, lunar debris is there for good. It will keep falling, keep hitting, keep creating more debris.

The real kick is that we're building moon bases without any way to track what's already there or predict where the next impact will be. There's no registry. There's no international agreement on who cleans up. There's just optimism and hope that nothing critical gets hit before someone figures out the rules.
