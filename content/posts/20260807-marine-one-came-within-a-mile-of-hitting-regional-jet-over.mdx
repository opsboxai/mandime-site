The presidential helicopter and a regional jet nearly occupied the same airspace over Washington DC last month, separated by less than a mile vertically when they passed each other. Marine One, carrying either the President or a senior official, was descending into Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport when it crossed paths with a Bombardier regional jet climbing out on the same route. The two aircraft were on a collision course that the system designed to prevent exactly this kind of thing should have caught and resolved before they got anywhere close.

What makes this worse is that the Marine Corps, which operates Marine One, told the FAA the whole thing was routine. They called it a standard operation, nothing to worry about, move along. Except it wasn't routine. According to the incident report, the two aircraft came within a mile of each other, which is well inside the buffer that's supposed to exist between departing and arriving traffic at a major airport. At that distance and on converging paths, you're not talking about a near miss anymore. You're talking about a failure of the system that's supposed to make near misses impossible.

The fact that both incidents might be connected suggests this wasn't a one-off lapse but something structural about how Marine One operations interact with normal traffic control. The presidential helicopter gets priority routing and special treatment by design, but that privilege only works if everyone involved is actually communicating and coordinating. If the hand-off between controllers broke down, or if the Marine Corps wasn't following the same procedures as civilian traffic, you get situations where a helicopter that's supposed to be getting special treatment instead becomes a hazard to everyone else in the sky. That's not routine. That's a problem.
