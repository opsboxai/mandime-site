Steve Eimers became something of an unlikely internet figure by documenting guardrails on TikTok and YouTube, but his pivot to calling out Flock license plate readers turned out to have real consequences. After he posted videos about two specific cameras installed near his home in North Carolina, both got destroyed within days. Someone didn't just smash them; they targeted the exact units he'd highlighted online.

The thing that makes this actually interesting isn't the vandalism itself. It's that Eimers has now stopped posting about the cameras entirely. Not because he got threatened or because the footage scared him off, but because he realized his platform created a direct line between his audience and infrastructure he was criticizing. He posted the locations. People watched. Cameras got wrecked. The causality is murky enough to stay legally safe, but clear enough that he got the message.

This exposes something real about the ALPR debate that rarely gets said out loud: there's a gap between discourse and action, and that gap is narrowing. License plate readers are genuinely controversial. Privacy advocates make solid arguments. But when you give thousands of people the exact GPS coordinates of a camera and a reason to be angry about it, you've created a situation where the conversation stops being theoretical. Eimers understood that distinction once his own videos became the catalyst.

The cameras themselves are coming back online, which means this wasn't about shutting down Flock's operation. It was about a specific creator's content serving as a blueprint for vandalism. That's different from just disagreeing with surveillance technology. That's when you realize you've accidentally made yourself part of something you didn't sign up to lead.
