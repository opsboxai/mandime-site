AI chatbots are vacuuming up everything you type. Your medical questions, your relationship problems, your half-formed business ideas, your late-night existential spirals—all of it gets stored, sometimes forever, often sold or leaked or handed over to law enforcement without a warrant. The companies running these services claim they're not reading your stuff. They're lying, or at best being technically true while missing the point entirely.

Here's what actually matters: the data you feed to ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini isn't disappearing into the cloud. It's sitting on servers owned by for-profit companies with no real incentive to keep it secret. Wired's Andy Greenberg walked through the actual mechanics of how to use these tools without turning your brain into a commodity. The gist is simple but requires friction. You need local AI models that run on your own hardware, encrypted connections, or at minimum, the discipline to never ask an AI anything you wouldn't want your insurance company or future employer to see.

The practical layer: tools like Ollama let you run language models locally without sending anything to the cloud. For cloud-based services, turn off chat history in settings. Use a VPN. Consider a separate email for AI experiments. It sounds paranoid until you realize that OpenAI's terms of service explicitly let them use your conversations to train future models unless you opt out. That's not paranoia. That's reading the fine print. The stuff Greenberg dug into shows that the privacy settings exist, but they're buried and often defaulted to off. You have to actively reclaim your own conversations. The annoying truth is that the easiest AI tools to use are the ones most likely to sell you out.
