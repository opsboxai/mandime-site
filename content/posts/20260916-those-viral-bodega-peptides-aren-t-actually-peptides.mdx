WIRED tested four types of peptides sold in bodegas and found that most of them aren't actually peptides at all. The lab results expose what happens when a supplement trend explodes faster than any regulatory body can keep up with it.

The peptide market has become one of those fringe-to-mainstream movements that happens almost overnight. You see them everywhere now, sold in corner stores next to energy drinks, marketed as performance boosters and anti-aging shortcuts. The appeal is obvious: the science behind real peptides is legitimate, which makes the sales pitch easy. But here's the catch. The FDA doesn't pre-approve supplements the way it does drugs. Manufacturers can pretty much label and sell whatever they want, and by the time anyone runs actual tests, thousands of people have already bought the stuff.

What WIRED found matters because it's not just about false advertising. If these bottles don't contain what they claim, you're paying for snake oil dressed up in biotech language. Worse, you have no idea what's actually in there. That's not a minor issue when people are injecting or ingesting random compounds hoping for results. The lightly regulated peptide industry is basically operating on an honor system that clearly isn't working. Until the regulatory framework catches up to the hype, the safest move is skepticism. Real peptides exist and have real effects. The ones from your bodega probably don't.
