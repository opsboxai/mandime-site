Zuckerberg published 6,500 words on AI and somehow managed to say almost nothing. That's not hyperbole. The Meta CEO's recent manifesto dropped with the weight of something meant to shape the conversation around artificial intelligence, but crack it open and you get a lot of vague assertions dressed up as philosophy. Open source good. Closed source bad. AI will change everything. None of it lands with any real specificity or, more importantly, any actual skin in the game.

What makes this interesting isn't what he wrote. It's what his needing to write it tells you about where Silicon Valley is right now. These lengthy AI position papers have become the tech equivalent of a regulatory filing or a preemptive legal defense. They exist to stake territory, to sound thoughtful when you're actually making money moves, and to say something vague enough that you can't be pinned down later. Zuckerberg wants open source to win because Meta's business model works better in a world where the base technology is commodified. That's not a manifesto. That's a business strategy dressed in philosophy robing.

The real tells come in what he sidesteps entirely. Nothing about labor displacement. Nothing concrete about how Meta's own models will be governed. Nothing about the actual economic winners and losers this tech creates. Just a lot of "AI will be great and everyone should have access to it," which sounds nice until you realize that "everyone" in tech speak means "everyone who can build on our infrastructure." It's the kind of position that lets you claim the moral high ground while your balance sheet does the actual work.
