Toyota's new playbook is to make their trucks look almost identical and then let the chassis do the talking. The 2027 Tundra and Sequoia share so much DNA that at a glance they're basically the same vehicle, except one hauls people and the other hauls, well, also people but with more space. But here's what matters: both can genuinely off-road at a level that makes most truck owners look like they're playing in a sandbox.

Collin Woodard got sent to jump these things, which is the kind of assignment that separates the "I own a truck" guys from the people who actually know what trucks do. The TrailHunter and TRD Pro variants are where Toyota stops playing it safe. We're talking multi-link rear suspension, electronic locking differentials, crawl control that works, and geometry that doesn't have to apologize to anyone. The Sequoia gets the three-row cabin advantage, but mechanically it's operating on the same level of capable. When you're talking about this class of full-size off-roader, that capability spread used to matter a lot more than it does now.

The thing Toyota figured out is that if you engineer the chassis right, the body style becomes secondary. Both trucks can handle terrain that would make a Wrangler driver nervous, which ten years ago would have sounded insane coming out of a truck that also does school runs and towing. That's not a coincidence. It's purpose-built engineering that costs money, and Toyota decided the money was worth spending. Most truck buyers will never jump these things off a berm, but knowing they could changes how you feel about ownership.
