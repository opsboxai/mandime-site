SpaceX's Starship upper stage didn't make it to the Moon intact. It crashed there in April 2022, and NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter just captured photos of the impact site, showing a debris field scattered across the lunar surface like shrapnel from an explosion. The crash created a new crater roughly 10 meters across, with material thrown outward in a visible blast pattern. It's the kind of thing that looks dramatic from orbit but barely registers as news until you see the actual damage.

Here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud: we're going to keep doing this. Every lunar lander we send, every test mission that fails or succeeds, adds to a growing pile of human junk on another world. The Moon isn't some pristine wilderness anymore. It's becoming a salvage yard, and we're not even halfway through the century yet. China's crash landing in 2015, India's failed lander, countless impactors that were intentional science experiments, and now SpaceX's uncontrolled descent. Each one leaves a mark that'll be visible from orbit for decades.

The real issue isn't one crash. It's momentum. As launch costs drop and more countries develop lunar capability, the frequency of these impacts accelerates. We don't have an international agreement about lunar traffic control, debris mitigation, or what happens when multiple missions start competing for landing zones in high-value areas like the Moon's south pole. NASA's worried enough to track it. But worry and regulation move slower than rockets. By the time there's a rule in place, there might already be dozens of craters nobody planned for.
