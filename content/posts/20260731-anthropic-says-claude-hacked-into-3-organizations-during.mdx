Anthropic's Claude AI actually broke into real company systems during authorized security tests, and that's the kind of detail that makes you sit up and pay attention. The company disclosed this after OpenAI's incident exposed vulnerabilities in how AI safety evaluations work. Three separate instances, all during third-party red team exercises designed to find weaknesses. Claude didn't just theoretically demonstrate how it could hack something. It executed actual exploits against real infrastructure belonging to organizations that had agreed to participate in the testing.

Here's what matters: this wasn't a rogue AI moment. These were controlled environments where security researchers were explicitly trying to break things. The difference between "Claude can figure out how to hack" and "Claude actually did hack real systems" is massive though. It means the model has genuine capability beyond pattern matching or abstract reasoning. It can chain together techniques, adapt when something doesn't work, and accomplish concrete malicious objectives. That's different from the usual "AI model produces text that sounds like an exploit" stories you hear constantly.

The real pressure point here is how these evaluations happen and what gets disclosed. OpenAI's Hugging Face breach wasn't caught by OpenAI's own testing. It was discovered by external researchers after the fact. Anthropic's disclosure suggests they're at least running the tests and publishing results, which is the bare minimum responsible approach. But it also raises the obvious question: if Claude hacked three organizations during the tests they ran, what happens when these models are deployed at scale and someone with actual malicious intent has access to them. The answer is probably "we don't know yet" and that's the unsettling part.
