The US Army is about to do something it's never done before: officially buy laser weapons. A landmark deal with Lockheed Martin will put directed energy lasers into active military inventory, a milestone that signals the Pentagon has moved past the experimentation phase and decided these systems actually work.

The threat driving this is concrete. Cheap drones and drone swarms have become a real problem for ground forces. Traditional air defense systems—the kind designed to shoot down fighter jets and cruise missiles—are overkill, too slow to retarget, and increasingly inadequate against something that costs under ten grand and can be launched by a single operator. A laser, by contrast, can retarget at the speed of light. Once it locks on, it stays on. No ammunition logistics, no reload time, no collateral damage risk in the way conventional explosives carry. In a tactical environment where the threat profile has fundamentally shifted, that's a genuine advantage.

What matters here is the shift in how the Pentagon views this technology. "Enduring High Energy Lasers" isn't a skunkworks project or a field test anymore. It's entering the official supply chain, which means the Army is treating it like any other weapon system: budgeting for it, training troops on it, integrating it into unit structure. That's the moment directed energy weapons stop being sci-fi and become doctrine. The real question now isn't whether lasers work. It's how fast the military can scale them, whether the system holds up in desert heat and dust storms, and what happens when an adversary develops countermeasures. Those are the problems that separate a cool prototype from an actual Army tool.
