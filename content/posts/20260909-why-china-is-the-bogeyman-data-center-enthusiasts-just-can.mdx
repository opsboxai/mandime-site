Americans have decided they hate data centers. Polling numbers are staggering: majorities across political lines don't want them in their backyards, don't trust who's running them, and don't believe the promised economic benefits. But here's the thing—tech industry leaders and their allies have found a workaround. Instead of fixing the actual problems people care about (land use, power consumption, environmental impact, local control), they've pivoted to a simpler argument: you should want data centers because China wants them more.

The pitch is seductive and vague at once. China's building massive compute infrastructure. China's investing in AI. China's getting ahead. And if we don't build our own data centers fast and loose, the reasoning goes, we lose. The problem is that when you actually dig into the specifics, the evidence isn't there. There's no hard data showing Chinese data centers are outpacing American ones in any strategically meaningful way, no proof that relaxing environmental standards or local zoning laws in rural America gives us a competitive edge, no real accounting of what we'd be trading away. It's a fear play dressed up as strategy.

What makes it work is that it bypasses the actual conversation. Nobody has to answer why a small town in Iowa should sacrifice groundwater access so a hyperscaler can run margin-thin AI training cheaper. Nobody has to reckon with whether unlimited data center growth is actually good infrastructure policy or just good for a specific set of companies. The China argument lets politicians and executives skip past the friction entirely. Voters get reframed as unpatriotic if they object. Local concerns become obstructions in a great power competition. And the people making decisions get to avoid ever having to prove their case on its actual merits.
