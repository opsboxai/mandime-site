Red light therapy has gone from fringe biohacking thing to something you can buy at Target, which means it's time to actually figure out if it works or if you're just paying money to sit under a lamp that looks like a spaceship prop.

The science here isn't totally made up. Red and near-infrared wavelengths do penetrate skin, and there's legitimate research showing they can boost mitochondrial function and increase blood flow to treated areas. The catch is that most studies are small, many are funded by the companies selling the devices, and the gap between "shows promise in a lab" and "will noticeably change your face" is still pretty wide. For muscle recovery, the evidence is mixed at best. For skin, you're looking at modest improvements in collagen production and wound healing, not the kind of transformation that Instagram would have you believe. Hair regrowth studies exist, but they're mostly on people using clinical-grade equipment, not your $200 panel.

Here's the real problem: red light therapy works best when it's part of an actual routine. It's not a standalone solution. Better sleep, consistent exercise, not eating like garbage, and basic skincare still matter more. If you're already doing those things and you want to throw a red light panel into the mix, the downside is minimal and the upside is probably real, just smaller than the marketing suggests. But if you're buying it as a shortcut to looking better without changing anything else, you're essentially paying for permission to feel like you're doing something.

The devices themselves range from legitimately effective to barely better than a red Christmas light. The ones that work tend to cost real money. The cheap ones might do something, or might just look cool on your nightstand.
