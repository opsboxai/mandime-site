Gordon Murray just dropped the S1, and it's the kind of car that makes you remember why he matters. After spending the last decade chasing electric efficiency and modular design philosophy, Murray went back to first principles: a sub-900kg driver's car with a naturally aspirated 3.5-liter V12 that screams to 11,000 rpm. No hybrid battery pack. No turbo lag. Just raw mechanical feedback at a weight that hasn't been feasible in a road car since the '90s.

The numbers alone are almost absurd. 740 horsepower, manual gearbox, and a power-to-weight ratio that puts it in hypercar territory while pricing it like a high-spec GT. But the real story is simpler: Murray built this because he could, and because nobody else would. At 75, after designing the McLaren F1 and reinventing how we think about lightweight cars, he's made something that feels like a direct line back to what he learned 30 years ago. The S1 doesn't need to be revolutionary. It just needs to be absolutely, brutally pure.

What makes it land differently than the endless parade of million-pound supercars is the constraint. Murray chose to limit himself. No fancy suspension wizardry, no hybrid complexity, no electronic assistance unless absolutely necessary. The result is a car that feels like it exists specifically to prove a point: that sometimes the smartest engineering decision is to do less, not more. Only 25 will be built. That's not scarcity marketing. That's the number Murray decided was right.
