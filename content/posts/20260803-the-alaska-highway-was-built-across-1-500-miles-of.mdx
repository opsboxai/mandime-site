After Pearl Harbor, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers had eight months to carve 1,500 miles of driveable road through Canadian wilderness to Alaska. They did it.

The Alaska Highway, now called the Alcan, was a proposal that had floated around since the 1920s. Canada kept saying no. The idea made sense on paper—link Alaska to the continental U.S., open up resources, strengthen sovereignty—but the cost was brutal and the terrain worse. Then December 7th happened, and suddenly the entire calculus changed. Japan had just proved it could strike American territory. Alaska, sitting exposed at the top of the continent with barely any defensive infrastructure, became strategically critical overnight. Canada got it. The U.S. got it. The project went from theoretical to urgent.

What happened next is genuinely wild. The Corps of Engineers mobilized 10,000 soldiers and civilian workers, scattered them across one of the harshest stretches of geography on the continent, and told them to connect Dawson Creek in British Columbia to Delta Junction in Alaska before winter made the whole thing impossible. No GPS. No modern surveying tools. No real maps of half the territory. Just bulldozers, determination, and the understanding that you move fast or you don't move at all. They averaged 6 to 8 miles per day. By October 1942, they'd finished. A road that should have taken years was complete in months because failure meant leaving Alaska defenseless and Japan with an open invitation.

The highway still exists, still matters, and most people have no idea it was built under wartime pressure by soldiers who were essentially told to make a road through the impossible.
