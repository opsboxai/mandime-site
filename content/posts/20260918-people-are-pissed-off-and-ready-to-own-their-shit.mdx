Right-to-repair isn't some fringe tech complaint anymore. It's turning into a real movement, and Louis Rossmann just proved it by taking a grassroots live tour across the country to talk about why people can't fix their own stuff.

The anger is legit. You buy a phone or laptop, something breaks, and suddenly you're locked out. The manufacturer won't sell you parts. Independent repair shops get sued for trying to help. Farmers can't fix their own tractors without a dealer's permission. It's control masquerading as protection, and people are done with it. Rossmann, who's spent years calling out Apple's repair tactics on YouTube, decided to hit the road and turn that frustration into something tangible. Not a petition. Not an online campaign. An actual tour where he could sit in a room with people who are genuinely tired of being locked out of the things they own.

What makes this different is that it's working. The turnout proved something the tech world has been slow to admit: right-to-repair matters to regular people, not just hackers and tinkerers. It matters because fixing something yourself costs less, takes less time, and feels like you actually own the device. It matters because planned obsolescence is just planned waste. And it matters because the alternative is a world where every piece of hardware becomes a rental you technically buy once but can never truly control.

Companies are starting to feel it. Apple finally announced a self-repair program. Other manufacturers are watching. But this isn't over because Rossmann moved on. This is over when the law catches up, when parts and repair manuals become normal, when owning something means you can actually fix it. The tour just reminded everyone that enough people want that badly enough to show up and say so.
