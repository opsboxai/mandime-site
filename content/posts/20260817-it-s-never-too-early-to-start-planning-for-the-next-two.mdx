If you watched the 2024 solar eclipse and thought that was it, you're already behind. The next two total solar eclipses are coming in 2027 and 2028, and they're supposed to be even longer than what most people just saw. We're talking about serious totality windows, the kind where you can actually see the sun's corona in detail without rushing through it.

Here's the catch: the best viewing spots fill up fast. Hotels book years in advance. Roads clog. Airports become chaos. If you want a front-row seat to either of these events, you need to start planning now, not in 2026 when everyone else decides it's time to book. The 2024 eclipse taught a hard lesson about waiting until the last minute. People drove for eight hours to reach a location and hit traffic they didn't expect. Some never made it.

The 2027 eclipse crosses Spain and North Africa, hitting regions that are more accessible and developed than some of the 2024 path zones, but that accessibility works both ways. Easier to get there means more people will try. The 2028 eclipse tracks across Australia, which has its own appeal and its own complications. If you're serious about seeing two back-to-back totalities, start mapping accommodation and travel routes now. Look at the path maps, check what the weather patterns typically look like in those regions during those times, and figure out your backup plan if clouds roll in. Your 2027 self will thank you for the legwork you do today.
