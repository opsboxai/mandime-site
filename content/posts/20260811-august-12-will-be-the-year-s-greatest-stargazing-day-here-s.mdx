The Perseid meteor shower peaks on August 12, and this year conditions are almost perfect. No moon means the sky stays dark, so you're looking at upward of 100 meteors per hour if you're in the right spot. That's not a typo. That's the kind of night where you actually see the sky doing something.

Most people mess this up by staying in the city or waiting until midnight. Get out past the light pollution, find some elevation if you can, and start watching around 10 or 11 p.m. The meteors will keep coming until dawn, but the rate picks up as the night deepens and the radiant point climbs higher. Bring a blanket, kill your phone's brightness, and let your eyes adjust for at least 20 minutes. You don't need binoculars or a telescope. You just need darkness and patience.

The Perseids happen every August because Earth passes through the debris trail left by Swift-Tuttle comet. This particular year the new moon falls right at peak, which doesn't happen often. If you're going to pick one night to step outside and actually look up, make it this one.
