There's a category of cooking that sits somewhere between bushcraft and restaurant technique, and it's older than both. Caveman steak, also called coal-roasted steak, strips away everything between you and fire. You salt your meat, let it sit, then drop a rib eye straight into the hot coals and walk away. No grates. No thermometer obsession. Just meat and heat doing what they've done for thousands of years.

The method sounds reckless until you realize why it works. The coals create a fast, intense crust that seals in juice while the residual heat cooks the inside through gentle carryover. The ash itself becomes insulation, preventing the outside from charring to carbon while the center stays rare. It's the opposite of the precision-focused grilling culture that's been selling us $3,000 pellet smokers for the last decade. Eisenhower didn't need a meat thermometer to know when his steak was done, and neither do you.

The practical reality is that this only works with decent meat and respect for the fire. You need coals hot enough to sear instantly, not a lukewarm pile of ash. The cut matters too, thick and substantial enough to survive the direct heat without drying out. A thin flank steak dies here. A two-inch rib eye thrives. And yes, you'll eat some ash. That's not a bug. Most people who've done this once end up doing it again, which tells you something about the gap between food media's complexity and what actually tastes good when you're standing next to fire with a cold drink in your hand.
