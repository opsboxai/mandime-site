Flock Safety sells surveillance cameras to cops as a straightforward tool: catch the bad guys by their license plates. Simple, useful, sold to thousands of police departments across the country. But WIRED got their hands on the actual code behind Flock's next-generation AI system, the one already deployed in active investigations, and it does something significantly darker than plate recognition.

The system can identify people by their appearance, their gait, their clothing patterns, even the specific way they walk. Flock built this into what they're calling their "Investigate" OS, and when WIRED reconstructed and tested it, the results were unambiguous. The company didn't market this capability prominently. Police departments didn't necessarily know they had it. The code was there anyway, running live on systems that are already being used to flag suspects in real investigations happening right now.

This matters because there's a massive gap between what surveillance companies advertise and what their systems actually do. License plate readers are controversial enough. But a tool that can flag someone based on how they move through the world, paired with the scale of Flock's camera network, changes the equation entirely. It's the difference between identifying a getaway car and identifying a person by their body. One is a lead. The other is identification. And if the system gets it wrong, which computer vision systems regularly do, that's a real person getting flagged, possibly detained, based on an algorithm's guess about their gait.

Flock has been operating in this space long enough to know exactly what they're building and how departments use it. The fact that they built the system first and explained it second, if at all, tells you something about how they expected pushback to land.
