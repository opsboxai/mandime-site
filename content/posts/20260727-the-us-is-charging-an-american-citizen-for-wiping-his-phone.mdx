Sam Tunick walked into Atlanta's airport on January 24th and walked out facing federal charges for allegedly giving federal agents a fake password that wiped his phone clean. The catch: he's an American citizen, and the government is charging him with obstructing justice for what amounts to hitting a self-destruct button on his own device.

Here's what actually happened. Border agents detained Tunick, questioned him about suspected child exploitation material, then demanded access to his phone. According to his legal team, when they tried to unlock it, Tunick provided what's called a "duress password" — essentially a security feature built into his device that wipes everything when entered. Federal prosecutors are now treating that as a crime, arguing he obstructed justice by destroying evidence.

The legal weirdness here runs deep. The government is claiming that using a built-in phone feature counts as evidence destruction, which would mean any phone manufacturer that enables such a feature is basically helping people commit federal crimes. It's also treating the destruction of data on a personal device as obstruction equivalent to, say, burning physical evidence you know law enforcement is about to seize. But here's the part that matters: without a warrant or a conviction proving those images actually existed on the phone, the prosecution is essentially charging him for erasing something they can't prove was ever there.

This isn't about defending what might have been on the device. It's about the government establishing that American citizens can be prosecuted for using security features on phones they own, during searches that happened without a warrant, at a border where constitutional protections get murky anyway. If this prosecution sticks, it sets a precedent that basically says the government can charge you for protecting your own data.
