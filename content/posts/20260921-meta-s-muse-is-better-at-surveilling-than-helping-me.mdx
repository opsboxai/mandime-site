Meta just quietly rolled out an app that asks for your bank account, email, and passport number before it even tries to help you. The Muse app, which ostensibly uses AI to answer questions about your finances, is less of a tool and more of a data harvesting operation dressed up as a productivity assistant. Reece Rogers tested it for Wired and found the real product wasn't financial advice. It was you.

Here's the pattern: Meta builds something useful-sounding, makes it free, then systematically nudges you toward handing over sensitive information that feeds their AI training pipeline. Muse does this with a particular efficiency. The app doesn't need your passport to answer a question about whether you can afford rent. It doesn't require bank access to give generic budgeting tips. It wants these things because they're valuable training data, and because the company has repeatedly shown it will extract whatever it can from users who don't read the fine print.

The surveillance angle here isn't even hidden anymore. Meta has spent years getting users comfortable with the idea that free tools come with a data cost. But there's a difference between the platform knowing what you click on and the platform holding your financial identity. Muse collapses that boundary entirely. It's Meta's way of saying: we've already got your social graph, your photos, your messages. Now we want your money too.
