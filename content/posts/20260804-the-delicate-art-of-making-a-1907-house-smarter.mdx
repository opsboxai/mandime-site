Jordan Michelman bought a 1907 house and immediately wanted to wire it for the 21st century without gutting what made it worth buying in the first place. That tension is the actual problem nobody talks about when they say "smart home." It's not about the tech. It's about fitting a nervous system into a body that was never meant to have one.

The practical nightmare is obvious: a 1907 house has plaster walls that don't take kindly to drilling, electrical panels that can't handle extra load, and crown molding that will murder you if you try to run conduit through it. But the real challenge is subtler. Every smart light, every connected lock, every sensor you add is a decision to compromise on original details for convenience you might not actually need. Michelman's approach was surgical. He tested modern locks that bolt onto existing hardware instead of replacing it, light switches that fit into original plates, and climate controls that hide in plain sight. The goal wasn't to make the house futuristic. It was to make it functional without making it look like a tech demo.

What makes this work is restraint. Not every room needs to be smart. Not every system needs to talk to every other system. The 1907 house wins when you treat automation as a utility, not a lifestyle choice. A smart thermostat makes sense because you're not installing new ductwork anyway. A connected lock makes sense if the alternative is rekeying the whole house. But if you're running Cat-6 through historic walls just so your lights can sync with your music, you've already lost the argument.

The real insight here is that old houses taught us something we've forgotten: constraint makes you smarter. A 1907 house forces you to ask what you actually need instead of what's possible. That discipline is worth more than any integration protocol.
